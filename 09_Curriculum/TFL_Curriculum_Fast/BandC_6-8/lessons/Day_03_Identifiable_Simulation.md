# Band C — Day 3: Simulation Must Be Identifiable

**Duration:** 45 min
**Materials:** Notebooks, chart paper

---

## Objective

Students learn that `Sim(t)` requires stateable content, and that vague feelings do not qualify.

---

## 1. Hook — Two Statements (6 min)

Write both on the board:

```
A: "I had a bad feeling about the test."
B: "I expected question 4 to be about fractions."
```

**Ask:**
> "Which one is a simulation?"

Let them argue. Then resolve.

**Say:**
> "B. Only B. And this matters more than it sounds."

---

## 2. Name — The Identifiability Rule (12 min)

**Say:**
> "`Sim(t)` is a specific hypothesis about what comes next. Simulation content must be identifiable — you have to be able to state what it was."

Write the test:

```
THE TEST:
Can you say what you expected, specifically enough
that we could check whether it happened?

Yes → it's Sim(t)
No  → this model doesn't apply
```

**Say the important part:**
> "This is a boundary of the framework, not a rule about you. TFL applies only where simulation content is identifiable. A vague feeling isn't a failure of your memory — it's outside what the model covers."

---

## 3. Work — The Sharpening Exercise (16 min)

**Part A (8 min)** — Students write five vague statements about expectations they've had. Deliberately vague.

Examples: "I thought it'd go badly." "Something felt off."

**Part B (8 min)** — Students attempt to sharpen each into a specific `Sim(t)`.

```
Vague:     _____________________________
Sharpened: _____________________________
Checkable? Yes / No
```

**Key instruction:**
> "Some of these you won't be able to sharpen. That's the useful result. Mark those."

---

## 4. Compare — The Unsharpenable Ones (8 min)

Collect statements students could not sharpen.

**Ask:**
> "What should we do with these?"

**Say:**
> "Leave them out. A model that applies to everything explains nothing. TFL tells you where it applies, and this is one of the edges."

---

## 5. Carry (3 min)

**Say:**
> "Tomorrow: what decides whether a simulation survives into the next window. It's `E(t)`, and it works differently than people assume."

---

## Instructor Notes

- Students often resist discarding examples. Frame exclusion as precision, not loss.
- This lesson prevents the most common Band D error — building a study around an unmeasurable simulation.
