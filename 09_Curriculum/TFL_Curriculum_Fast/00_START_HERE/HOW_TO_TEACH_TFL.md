# How to Teach TFL

This is the central instructor guide. It covers method, not content.

## 1. The One-Sentence Version

Every band, every day, every activity comes back to a single sentence:

> What I notice, what I remember, and what I expect combine into right now — and right now shapes what comes next.

If a student can say that sentence and give an honest example, the core idea has landed. Everything else is precision.

## 2. Teach the Loop Before the Parts

The most common instructional error is teaching the five components as a list, then trying to assemble them into a loop at the end.

Do the reverse.

**Day 1 of every band establishes that the loop exists.** Students should leave the first session knowing that experience repeats and carries forward, even if they cannot yet name a single component.

The parts are then taught as answers to a question students already have: *what exactly is repeating?*

A list of five terms is forgettable. A loop that students have already felt is not.

## 3. Use Experience First, Language Second

TFL describes something happening in students constantly. This is a significant teaching advantage.

The order that works:

```
1. Create the experience      (run an activity)
2. Ask what happened          (student language)
3. Name it                    (TFL language)
4. Return to the experience   (re-describe using the new name)
```

Never invert steps 1 and 3. If you name `Sim(t)` before students have been caught mid-prediction, the term is vocabulary. If you name it after, the term is a label for something they just felt.

## 4. The Surprise Is the Instrument

Prediction is invisible when it succeeds. It becomes visible the moment it fails.

This is why almost every activity in this curriculum contains an engineered mismatch: a sound that stops early, a pattern that breaks, a story that ends wrong, a routine that changes.

The teaching move after any mismatch is always the same:

> "You were surprised. Surprised means you expected something. What did you expect?"

That question converts an invisible process into a reportable one. Use it constantly.

## 5. Separate Persistence From Accuracy Early and Often

Students of every age arrive assuming that a prediction which *feels* strong is a prediction which is *correct*.

TFL says something different. Affective intensity governs whether a prediction is carried into the next cycle. It does not determine whether the prediction was right.

Make this explicit:

- Predictions that were correct often fade immediately.
- Predictions that were wrong sometimes stay for hours.

Bands C and D assess this distinction directly. Bands A and B plant it informally, through noticing which surprises are still being talked about later in the week.

## 6. Match Formality to the Band

| Band | Notation | Language |
|---|---|---|
| A (K-2) | None | Notice, remember, expect |
| B (3-5) | Component names only | Notice, remember, expect, right now |
| C (6-8) | Variables introduced | `Input(t)`, `Minfo(t)`, `Sim(t)`, `E(t)`, `F(t)` |
| D (9-12) | Full equations | Integration function, persistence threshold |

Do not push notation downward. A second grader writing `Sim(t)` has learned handwriting, not TFL. A twelfth grader saying "the expecting part" has been under-taught.

## 7. Let Wrong Predictions Be Comfortable

Students will resist reporting predictions if being wrong carries social cost. The entire curriculum depends on honest prediction reporting, so the classroom norm must be established on day one:

> In this unit, a wrong prediction is data. It is the most useful thing you can give us.

Practical moves that support this:

- Model it. Predict out loud, be wrong, say so cheerfully.
- Never mark a prediction right or wrong for credit. Mark whether it was *recorded*.
- When a student is wrong, ask what they expected and why. Treat the reasoning as the deliverable.

Every rubric in this package scores reasoning and completeness, never prediction accuracy.

## 8. Handle the Emotion Component Carefully

`E(t)` is affective intensity. In TFL it is a simple intensity signal, not a full emotional model, and it functions only to determine whether a prediction persists.

Teach it that way. The classroom-safe framing is:

> How much it mattered — which decides how long it stuck around.

Boundaries for all bands:

- Use low-stakes examples: surprises, games, sports, weather, schedule changes.
- Do not solicit personal emotional history.
- Do not invite disclosure about distressing events.
- Do not frame persistent thoughts as a problem, symptom, or disorder.

If a student volunteers something personal and distressing, acknowledge it briefly, do not analyze it through TFL, and follow your school's standard support procedure. TFL is not a clinical or therapeutic model and nothing in this curriculum should be presented as one.

## 9. Teach the Boundaries as Content

What TFL does *not* model is part of the framework, not a disclaimer.

TFL is defined for short-timescale processing. It is not defined to model long-term memory consolidation, identity formation, narrative construction, developmental trajectories, or group-level dynamics. It does not specify neural circuits or anatomy. It does not claim to explain what consciousness is.

Band D assesses scope rejection directly: students must identify an invalid application, not only a valid one. Bands B and C plant it with a recurring question:

> "Is that something this model is for?"

A student who can say "that's outside what TFL covers" has understood the framework better than a student who applies it everywhere.

## 10. The Standard Lesson Shape

Every lesson plan in this package follows the same five-part shape. Once you have taught two lessons, you will know the rhythm.

```
1. Hook          3-5 min    An experience, usually a mismatch
2. Name          5-8 min    Attach TFL language to what just happened
3. Work          15-25 min  Students generate their own examples or traces
4. Compare       5-10 min   Surface disagreement, correct misconceptions
5. Carry         2-3 min    One sentence that sets up the next session
```

The Carry step is not optional. It is a deliberate instructional parallel to the framework itself: each lesson ends by propagating something forward into the next lesson.

## 11. Pacing Guidance

| Band | Sessions | Session length | Typical schedule |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 6 | 20-25 min | Two weeks, three per week |
| B | 8 | 30-40 min | Two to three weeks |
| C | 10 | 45 min | Three to four weeks |
| D | 12 | 50 min | Four to six weeks |

Micro-lessons are designed to be dropped into gaps, used as warm-ups, or used to re-enter the unit after a break. They do not require the main sequence.

## 12. If You Only Have One Session

Teach the loop, the surprise, and the carry-forward.

```
1. Run one prediction activity with an engineered mismatch.
2. Ask what they expected.
3. Name notice / remember / expect / right now.
4. Ask what the surprise made them expect next time.
```

That final question is the loop. A single well-run session that lands the carry-forward is worth more than six sessions of vocabulary.
