## Abstract

This manuscript introduces Simulation Synchronization Theory (SST), a neurocognitively grounded model of interpersonal interaction in which individuals project affectively encoded role simulations into the social environment. These simulations—comprising identity expectations, behavioral templates, and emotionally weighted narratives—interact with external feedback through recursive loops that shape self-concept, perception, and behavior over time. SST posits that the self is not static, but emerges through the synchronization of internal simulations with social feedback. Affective intensity governs simulation persistence, while feedback valence modulates whether projected roles are confirmed, revised, or rejected. Identity stabilizes when simulation loops resolve through environmental alignment, internal reinterpretation, or distributed social confirmation.

To formalize this process, a symbolic loop model is introduced—{S(t), R(t), E(t), F(t), A(t)}—representing Simulation, Role, Environmental feedback, Feedback valence, and Affective weight. Drawing from affective neuroscience, predictive social cognition, and memory reconsolidation research, SST outlines structured observational cases and offers testable predictions across social behavior and identity dynamics. The manuscript is structured according to the Brisart Format, with sections detailing the core mechanism (2.0), internal processing dynamics (3.0), predictive applications (4.0), and future directions (5.0). SST provides a falsifiable, affectively modulated account of how internal representations become socially enacted realities.

## 1.0 Introduction

Simulation Synchronization Theory (SST) proposes that identity stabilization and affective coherence emerge through the recursive alignment of internal simulations with external feedback. As individuals navigate the social world, they engage in ongoing simulations of how they are perceived, evaluated, and responded to. These simulations are continually updated based on environmental cues, forming a feedback loop between internal narrative construction and external social validation (Dereito et al., 2021; Foerde & Shohamy, 2011).

At its core, SST posits that the self is not a static entity, but a fluid simulation that synchronizes with observed feedback in order to minimize affective prediction error. The theory builds upon affective neuroscience, predictive coding, and identity theory to formalize how individuals form a stable sense of self under conditions of affective ambiguity or social complexity (Grossberg, 2002; Millidge et al., 2021).

Whereas existing models of self-regulation often emphasize cognitive control or behavioral reinforcement, SST introduces a novel mechanism grounded in recursive affective simulation. It theorizes that social identity is stabilized not through top-down goal structures alone, but through bottom-up affective resonance and synchronization of predicted versus observed emotional responses.

SST has immediate implications for understanding identity disorders, social anxiety, conformity, and the formation of group-based identities. It also offers a framework for experimental investigation using neuroimaging, psychophysiological tracking, and predictive error modeling (Skottnik et al., 2021; Tsao et al., 2018). By treating the social self as a dynamically simulated and affectively tuned model, SST provides a testable pathway for quantifying how real-time feedback modulates internal identity construction.

## 2.0 Core Mechanism

At the center of Simulation Synchronization Theory lies a simple but powerful principle: the self is a simulated narrative continuously adjusted through external emotional feedback. When individuals engage in social behavior, they internally generate simulations—predictions about how others will perceive, react to, or emotionally respond to their actions. These predictions are not neutral; they are affectively charged and encode expectations about approval, rejection, affinity, or threat (Foerde & Shohamy, 2011; Skottnik et al., 2021).

SST formalizes this process into a recursive feedback loop consisting of the following five components:

### 2.1 Affective Simulation Engine

The individual generates a forward-facing simulation of expected emotional outcomes based on context. This simulation is affective in nature—it encodes predicted emotional responses (e.g., warmth, disapproval) and anticipated shifts in relational dynamics. This mechanism is informed by prior experience and learned affective patterns (Grossberg, 2002).

### 2.2 Real-Time Feedback Sampling

As interaction occurs, real-time emotional cues—tone, expression, timing, and behavior—are sampled from the environment. These are decoded and assessed for congruence with the predicted outcome. The system is especially sensitive to disconfirmatory feedback, which may suggest that the internal model of self-presentation or social role is inaccurate (Dereito et al., 2021).

### 2.3 Prediction Error Detection

When the observed emotional feedback deviates from the predicted simulation, an affective prediction error is generated. This error signal is not purely cognitive—it is encoded in physiological and emotional responses (e.g., anxiety, shame, pride), directing attention to the mismatch and prompting model revision (Bi & Poo, 1998; Millidge et al., 2021).

### 2.4 Role Recalibration

The simulation engine adapts by updating the internal model of self-in-context. This includes not only beliefs about how others perceive the self, but also emotional valence associated with specific roles, expressions, and behaviors. Successful minimization of prediction error over time leads to the stabilization of a perceived social identity.

### 2.5 Synchronization Stabilization

Once simulations and feedback reach high congruence, the identity simulation becomes synchronized. The individual now experiences alignment between their internal role model and external validation. This state of low prediction error and emotional coherence is affectively reinforcing and becomes the new baseline for future social interactions (Caporale & Dan, 2008; Tang & Bassett, 2017).

SST proposes that identity is not merely a self-concept, but a simulation loop stabilized through affective convergence. When social feedback confirms predicted emotional outcomes, the simulation is reinforced. When it contradicts expectations, the system adapts. Through this recursive, emotionally weighted loop, the social self is constructed, refined, and synchronized.

## 3.0 Internal Processing Dynamics

Simulation Synchronization Theory (SST) identifies a set of neurocognitive dynamics that govern how simulated identities are constructed, tested, and stabilized through feedback. These internal dynamics are recursive, affect-laden, and prediction-driven, allowing the system to refine identity representations in real time.

### 3.1 Recursive Simulation Updating

Each time the individual anticipates a social interaction, a new iteration of the identity simulation is generated. This is not a copy of the prior model but a recursive update, incorporating both past experiences and new contextual inputs. The brain engages in an anticipatory modeling process similar to that described in predictive coding frameworks (Millidge et al., 2021), where the goal is to minimize future affective discrepancy.

### 3.2 Affective Weighting of Feedback

Not all feedback is treated equally. SST emphasizes that emotional salience modulates the updating process: emotionally intense or unexpected feedback carries greater weight and leads to stronger recalibration of the simulation engine (Skottnik et al., 2021). This prioritization allows for adaptive sensitivity to socially meaningful discrepancies.

### 3.3 Simulation Branching and Divergence

When prediction error is high, the simulation engine may create multiple parallel role simulations to explore how different expressions, tones, or behaviors might produce better alignment. This branching enables the system to iteratively select the version of the self that produces the most coherent and affectively reinforced outcome (Turner & Taverna, 2024).

### 3.4 Temporal Integration of Role Feedback

Role simulations are not evaluated in isolation. SST posits that feedback from one context is integrated across time, producing a temporally cumulative identity model. The lateral entorhinal cortex is implicated in such temporal integration processes (Tsao et al., 2018), allowing for stable trait-like roles to emerge from repeated interactions.

### 3.5 Role Compression and Identity Fixation

Once repeated simulations yield consistent, low-error outcomes across multiple contexts, the system compresses this pattern into a stabilized identity unit. This unit becomes resistant to further change, often forming the core of the person’s perceived social role. While stabilizing, this compression can also limit flexibility and contribute to maladaptive identity persistence in rigid environments (Ashwin et al., 2015).

Through these dynamics, SST offers a detailed account of how internal simulations adapt to and are shaped by the affective structure of social life. The identity that emerges is not static, but dynamically harmonized through recursive simulation, feedback comparison, and emotional weighting.

## 4.0 Applications and Predictions

Simulation Synchronization Theory (SST) offers a novel lens for interpreting a wide range of social phenomena, from identity formation to clinical symptoms. This section outlines structured observational patterns, each with an interpretation, empirical prediction, and illustrative example.

### 4.1 Identity Stabilization through Repeated Synchrony

- Interpretation: Repeated exposure to emotionally congruent feedback stabilizes an individual’s internal simulation loop, leading to the emergence of a consistent social identity.

- Prediction: Individuals exposed to environments with stable, affirming feedback will show reduced variability in self-concept over time and increased affective coherence during social tasks (Foerde & Shohamy, 2011).

- Example: A child consistently praised for kindness internalizes a prosocial identity, demonstrating low prediction error in future similar contexts.

### 4.2 Social Anxiety as Amplified Prediction Error

- Interpretation: Social anxiety reflects a hyperactive prediction error signal, where small deviations between expected and actual feedback are amplified affectively.

- Prediction: Individuals with social anxiety will exhibit heightened activity in brain regions associated with affective error detection (e.g., anterior cingulate cortex) in response to ambiguous social cues (Millidge et al., 2021).

- Example: A teenager replays an awkward interaction obsessively, overestimating the mismatch between their intended impression and perceived reception.

### 4.3 Conformity as Simulation Simplification

- Interpretation: Conformity behavior can emerge when the individual reduces simulation complexity by adopting dominant social feedback patterns to minimize error.

- Prediction: Under group pressure, role simulations become less divergent, with reduced neural signatures of branching prediction (Turner & Taverna, 2024).

- Example: A participant in an Asch-style line judgment task aligns with incorrect group consensus to maintain simulation alignment and reduce affective tension.

### 4.4 Dissociation and Feedback Decoupling

- Interpretation: Dissociation arises when internal simulations become uncoupled from real-time feedback due to overload, trauma, or conflicting roles.

- Prediction: Dissociative individuals will demonstrate diminished affective response to external social cues, despite continued internal simulation activity (Dereito et al., 2021).

- Example: A trauma survivor in a social setting smiles and responds appropriately but reports feeling disconnected or unreal.

### 4.5 Online Persona Fragmentation

- Interpretation: Maintaining multiple simulated identities across digital and physical platforms leads to fragmentation and feedback interference.

- Prediction: Users managing divergent online and offline personas will show increased prediction error and emotional fatigue when cross-contextual cues conflict (Skottnik et al., 2021).

- Example: An influencer with a curated online image experiences anxiety when recognized in public behaving incongruently with their digital persona.

### 4.6 Group Identity Entrenchment

- Interpretation: Group-based identities become entrenched when intra-group feedback loops reinforce shared simulations and suppress divergence.

- Prediction: Strong in-group affective feedback reduces neural simulation branching, leading to rigidity in social role expectations (Caporale & Dan, 2008).

- Example: A political group member resists moderating views due to the emotional reinforcement received from ideological alignment with peers.

### 4.7 Therapeutic Recalibration through Feedback Repatterning

- Interpretation: Psychotherapy can function by introducing novel emotional feedback patterns that update maladaptive simulations.

- Prediction: Successful therapeutic interventions will show a measurable shift in the client’s affective prediction maps, as indicated by changes in facial expressivity, language use, or neural activity over time (Grossberg, 2002; Bi & Poo, 1998).

- Example: A client with a rejection schema begins interpreting neutral feedback as non-threatening after repeated empathetic responses from a therapist.

These applications illustrate SST’s ability to bridge experimental, clinical, and social domains by making testable predictions grounded in affective neuroscience and real-time simulation dynamics.

## 5.0 Limitations and Future Work

While Simulation Synchronization Theory (SST) presents a coherent and empirically grounded model of identity formation through affective feedback loops, several limitations must be acknowledged.

### 5.1 Lack of Direct Neural Tracing

Although SST aligns with known mechanisms of predictive coding and affective modulation, it has not yet been validated through direct neural tracing studies. The recursive simulation-feedback loop proposed here is consistent with activity in areas such as the medial prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate, and entorhinal cortex (Tsao et al., 2018; Millidge et al., 2021), but the model remains theoretical until mapped via imaging, lesion, or stimulation methods.

### 5.2 Temporal Resolution Constraints

Identity simulations unfold rapidly, often below conscious awareness. SST relies on fast timescale affective processing, which current neuroimaging methods (e.g., fMRI) may not adequately capture. Future studies using techniques with higher temporal resolution—such as EEG or MEG—may better isolate prediction error signals during social feedback processing (Ashwin et al., 2015).

### 5.3 Ambiguity in Subjective Valence

Emotional responses to feedback are context-dependent and idiosyncratic. While SST provides a structure for how affect modulates simulation updating, it does not predict the valence or intensity of emotional responses in a universal way. This limitation may be addressed by incorporating individual difference variables or baseline emotional traits into future iterations of the model (Skottnik et al., 2021).

### 5.4 Role of Memory and Narrative Coherence

SST currently emphasizes real-time feedback synchronization but underrepresents the role of autobiographical memory in simulation persistence. Long-term identity stability likely depends on narrative coherence and memory integration, which may be incorporated into a future framework extension or hybrid model (Turner & Taverna, 2024).

### 5.5 Boundary Conditions and Pathological States

While SST accounts for typical identity development and adaptation, it has not yet been formally tested under conditions of identity disruption, such as dissociative identity disorder, schizophrenia, or extreme social isolation. Investigating how simulation loops break down or become maladaptive in these contexts is a critical direction for future empirical validation (Dereito et al., 2021).

### 5.6 Limited Cultural Modeling

Current SST formulations are based on predominantly Western psychological models and affective assumptions. Social feedback structures vary significantly across cultures, and simulation stabilization mechanisms may differ in collectivist versus individualist settings. Expanding SST cross-culturally will be vital to increasing its universality and explanatory depth (Foerde & Shohamy, 2011).

By recognizing these limitations, SST remains open to refinement, empirical testing, and interdisciplinary expansion. The framework offers a fertile structure for investigating how affective prediction, real-time simulation, and social feedback jointly shape the human self.

## 6.0 Final Synthesis

Simulation Synchronization Theory (SST) proposes that the human self is not a fixed entity, but a recursive simulation continuously updated through emotionally weighted feedback loops. This simulation is constructed in real time, predicted internally, tested socially, and refined based on affective congruence. When predictions about social-emotional responses align with actual feedback, the simulation stabilizes—and identity emerges.

SST integrates principles from predictive coding, affective neuroscience, and identity theory to model how emotional resonance drives identity formation and social role consolidation (Millidge et al., 2021; Dereito et al., 2021; Grossberg, 2002). It extends beyond static models of self-concept by positioning identity as a dynamically synchronized loop, modulated by recursive alignment between simulation and feedback.

This model reframes conformity, social anxiety, dissociation, and persona fragmentation as natural consequences of prediction error, role branching, or simulation misalignment. It also opens empirical pathways: identity coherence can be measured by tracking emotional prediction accuracy, feedback congruence, and simulation stability across contexts (Skottnik et al., 2021; Turner & Taverna, 2024).

At its core, SST offers a testable theory of how the social self emerges—not through abstract reflection, but through emotionally tuned simulation loops that seek resonance and reduce discord. The individual becomes who they believe others believe they are, mediated through recursive affect.

As a framework, SST is designed to evolve. Future work will expand its neuroanatomical specificity, explore its clinical applications, and test its predictions in both laboratory and naturalistic settings. But its central claim remains: identity is not possessed—it is performed, simulated, synchronized, and stabilized in affective space.

## Appendix A: Defined Terms and Concepts

This appendix defines key terms used throughout the Simulation Synchronization Theory (SST) framework to aid clarity, replication, and interdisciplinary interpretation.

**Affective Prediction Error**

The discrepancy between predicted and observed emotional responses during social interaction. In SST, this error functions as the primary updating signal for the identity simulation.

**Affective Simulation Engine**

The cognitive-affective mechanism responsible for generating internal simulations of expected emotional feedback in social contexts. It forecasts how others will feel or respond to one’s behavior or presence.

**Feedback Synchronization**

The alignment between internal role simulations and external emotional feedback. High synchronization indicates affective resonance; low synchronization triggers prediction error and model adjustment.

**Recursive Simulation Loop**

A repeated internal process where identity simulations are generated, tested against feedback, and revised. The loop is ongoing and adjusts over time based on accumulated feedback congruence.

**Role Calibration**

The adaptive process of modifying one’s internal identity simulation based on new social feedback. It allows for gradual alignment with external expectations or reinforcement patterns.

**Simulation Branching**

The creation of multiple identity variants in anticipation of feedback. The simulation engine tests various roles or behaviors internally to select the one most likely to yield positive emotional resonance.

**Synchronization Stabilization**

The state achieved when internal simulations consistently match external feedback, resulting in a low-error, emotionally reinforced identity pattern.

**Identity Compression**

The consolidation of repeated successful simulations into a fixed identity construct. This process enables efficient prediction but may reduce flexibility or lead to rigidity in social roles.

**Feedback Decoupling**

A breakdown in the synchronization loop, where simulations continue internally but are no longer anchored to external feedback. Common in dissociative states or emotional trauma.

**Emotional Salience Modulation**

The weighting of feedback based on emotional intensity. SST posits that highly affective feedback exerts greater influence on simulation recalibration than neutral feedback.

## Appendix B: Symbolic Loop Formalism

This appendix introduces a symbolic representation of the core feedback mechanism in Simulation Synchronization Theory (SST). The goal is to formalize how internal simulations and emotional feedback interact to stabilize identity through recursive synchronization.

### B.1 Core Loop Variables

Let the following symbols represent the major components:

- S_t: Internal identity simulation at time t

- F_t: External emotional feedback sampled at time t

- \hat{F}\_t: Predicted emotional feedback at time t

- \varepsilon_t: Affective prediction error at time t, computed as:
  \varepsilon_t = F_t - \hat{F}\_t

- \Delta S_t: Update to the simulation at time t, based on error signal

- \alpha: Learning rate (affective sensitivity or emotional weighting factor)

### B.2 Loop Dynamics

At each time step t, the system performs the following:

1.  Simulation Prediction:
    \hat{F}\_t = f(S_t)
    where f is a predictive function mapping the current simulation to expected emotional feedback.

2.  Error Calculation:
    \varepsilon_t = F_t - \hat{F}\_t
    The system detects emotional mismatch between expected and actual feedback.

3.  Simulation Update Rule:
    S\_{t+1} = S_t + \alpha \cdot \varepsilon_t
    The internal simulation is updated to reduce future error, with \alpha modulating how strongly feedback reshapes identity.

### B.3 Stability Criterion

When the prediction error approaches zero over successive iterations:

\lim\_{t \to \infty} \varepsilon_t \to 0

the simulation is said to be synchronized, and the identity enters a stable attractor state.

### B.4 Branching Simulations

Under conditions of high \varepsilon_t, the system may generate a set of alternative simulations:

\\ S_t^{(1)}, S_t^{(2)}, \dots, S_t^{(n)} \\

Each variant S_t^{(i)} is evaluated based on its projected prediction error. The simulation with the lowest expected \varepsilon is adopted as the new baseline:

S\_{t+1} = \arg\min_i \left\| F_t - f(S_t^{(i)}) \right\|

### B.5 Compression Function

To formalize long-term identity stabilization, define a compression function:

C(S\_{t-n}, \dots, S_t) \to S^\*

where S^\* is a compressed, trait-like identity construct representing stable simulation convergence across time.

This symbolic loop captures the recursive structure of SST, allowing for modeling, simulation, and potential implementation in computational cognitive architectures or experimental prediction pipelines.

## Appendix C: Experimental Testing Paradigms

This appendix outlines experimental designs to empirically test the predictions of Simulation Synchronization Theory (SST). Each paradigm targets a specific subsystem of the SST loop and is designed to be implementable using common cognitive neuroscience tools.

### C.1 Social Feedback Adaptation Task

Goal: Measure real-time identity simulation updating in response to varying emotional feedback.

- Design: Participants engage in a task where they present short video or text self-descriptions and receive randomized emotional feedback (positive, negative, neutral) from simulated peers.

> Measurement:

- EEG/ERP: Monitor prediction error components (e.g., feedback-related negativity).

- Self-report: Identity coherence ratings pre- and post-feedback.

- fMRI (optional): Track medial prefrontal cortex and anterior cingulate activation during feedback processing.

<!-- -->

- Hypothesis: Larger deviations from predicted feedback yield higher affective prediction error and stronger neural responses; repeated congruent feedback leads to simulation stabilization.

### C.2 Identity Branching Under Ambiguity

Goal: Detect simulation branching under socially ambiguous conditions.

- Design: Subjects are asked to imagine how different audiences (e.g., friend vs. supervisor) would react to a series of social behaviors.

> Measurement:

- fMRI: Compare activation in simulation-related regions (e.g., precuneus, temporoparietal junction).

- Behavioral: Number and complexity of identity variants generated.

- Language analysis: Degree of role differentiation in verbal or written responses.

<!-- -->

- Hypothesis: Ambiguous social contexts will evoke multiple distinct simulation paths; branching magnitude will correlate with perceived social risk.

### C.3 Emotional Feedback Learning Rate Calibration

Goal: Quantify the affective learning rate \alpha used in simulation updating.

- Design: Participants receive feedback on performance or likability across repeated rounds of a task, with feedback skewed positively, negatively, or neutrally.

> Measurement:

- Trial-by-trial adaptation in self-evaluation scores.

- Computational modeling: Fit a delta-rule learning model to estimate individual \alpha.

<!-- -->

- Hypothesis: Higher emotional sensitivity (e.g., trait anxiety) will correlate with higher \alpha, leading to faster and more volatile simulation updates.

### C.4 Dissociation and Feedback Decoupling Paradigm

Goal: Test SST’s prediction that dissociation arises from disrupted simulation-feedback coupling.

- Design: Compare two groups (high vs. low dissociation, measured via DES-II scale) on a modified social feedback task.

> Measurement:

- Skin conductance / heart rate variability.

- Eye-tracking: Gaze fixation on emotional feedback cues.

- fMRI: Reduced activation in salience and simulation networks.

<!-- -->

- Hypothesis: High-dissociation participants will exhibit lower physiological and neural responsiveness to feedback despite intact simulation behavior.

### C.5 Longitudinal Identity Stabilization Tracking

Goal: Observe identity convergence over time in naturalistic settings.

- Design: Participants maintain a digital diary for 30–60 days, recording social interactions and perceived emotional feedback.

> Measurement:

- Natural language processing: Extract self-referential patterns.

- Affective valence tagging of entries.

- Clustering analysis: Detect convergence toward dominant identity descriptors.

<!-- -->

- Hypothesis: Repeated emotionally congruent interactions will lead to reduced self-descriptive variability and increased identity confidence.

These paradigms provide a foundation for empirical validation of SST and offer modular pathways for lab testing across neuroimaging, behavioral, and computational domains. Each is designed to test a core mechanism in isolation or combination, depending on the desired resolution and available resources.
