## Abstract

Residual Identity Echo Theory (RIET) proposes that many ghost and apparition reports can be modeled as *Ghost Perception Events* (GPEs): brief, non-volitional, and typically non-interactive perceptual episodes in which an observer experiences a location-anchored “presence” or sensory fragment (visual, auditory, somatic) that is interpreted as a ghost. RIET is a neurocognitive framework centered on affective memory encoding, contextual reinstatement, and predictive processing, with an explicit empirical program designed to differentiate cue-driven accounts from stronger environmental-persistence hypotheses.

RIET is structured as a two-tier model. RIET-M (minimal) explains GPEs through ordinary mechanisms (cueing, emotional tagging, source-monitoring error, and inference under uncertainty). RIET-E (extended) is evaluated only if residual target-site effects replicate under cue-matching, verified blinding, and (where applicable) sham control (RQ4–RQ5; Section 5.3).

The framework generates preregisterable predictions (e.g., cue- and state-dependent report rates, replicable micro-location clustering, and attenuation under narrative blinding). Any environmental-persistence claim is treated as conditional on the RIET-E Decision Gate.

## 1.0 Introduction

Reports of apparitions and “felt presence” experiences are widespread across cultures and historical periods, yet they are often treated as either evidence for supernatural agency or as trivial misperceptions. A more neutral interpretation is that these accounts reflect a heterogeneous class of anomalous perceptual events that can be studied empirically—especially when they show recurring phenomenology (e.g., sensed presence, fragmentary visuals/auditory cues) and predictable moderators such as sleep-wake boundary states or environmental ambiguity (Persinger, 2001; Sharpless & Barber, 2011; Wiseman et al., 2003). On this view, the scientific question is not whether ghosts exist, but which measurable mechanisms reliably produce ghost-like experiences under specific contextual conditions.

Existing explanations typically cluster into two broad families:

- **Survivalist/parapsychological interpretations:** culturally influential but typically lacking preregisterable mechanism discriminators.

<!-- -->

- **Neurocognitive and environmental accounts:** hallucination/suggestion mechanisms, sleep-related phenomena (e.g., isolated sleep paralysis), and environmental factors such as CO/CO<sub>2</sub>, flicker, and infrasound (Sharpless & Barber, 2011; Tandy & Lawrence, 1998; Wiseman et al., 2003).

However, these accounts are often applied post hoc and do not always explain why reports sometimes cluster within specific micro-locations or why certain phenomenological motifs recur across observers.

Residual Identity Echo Theory (RIET) treats many ghost reports as structured episodes of anomalous perception. In RIET-M, intense affective experiences are strongly encoded and later reconstructed when stable place cues and uncertainty reinstate aspects of the original context, biasing predictive inference and source monitoring.

RIET builds on two well-established findings: (1) emotional arousal strengthens consolidation and the later vividness of reconstructed episodes, and (2) stable environmental context cues bias retrieval and reconstruction. Together, these mechanisms make it plausible that place cues plus uncertainty can repeatedly elicit similar, compelling reconstructions that observers interpret as apparitions (Dolcos et al., 2005; McGaugh, 2015; Phelps, 2006; Smith & Vela, 2001).

RIET conceptualizes these episodes as Ghost Perception Events (GPEs): non-volitional, typically non-interactive perceptual experiences shaped by contextual reinstatement and predictive inference. GPEs are usually temporally fragmented, emotionally salient, and spatially constrained within a site.

### 1.1 Research Questions and Confirmatory Claims

RIET is organized around explicit research questions with preregistered outcomes. RIET-M and RIET-E are evaluated as nested models fit to the same data, with criteria for when additional assumptions are warranted.

- **RQ1 (Measurement):** Can GPEs be reliably measured under standardized conditions?

  - **Confirmatory support:** acceptable inter-rater reliability for checklist scoring, stable base rates under neutral framing, and consistent within-subject baselines.

  - **Disconfirming outcome:** reports are too inconsistent to score reliably or collapse entirely into generic misperception/panic markers.

- **RQ2 (Spatial specificity):** Do within-site micro-location clusters replicate above chance?

  - **Confirmatory support:** permutation-tested clustering that replicates across independent samples and is stronger in targets than cue-matched controls.

  - **Disconfirming outcome:** flat spatial distributions and non-replicating “hotspots.”

- **RQ3 (Expectancy attenuation):** How much variance is explained by narrative priming and demand characteristics?

  - **Confirmatory support:** measurable attenuation under blinding plus remaining cue-linked variance explainable by RIET-M state×trait×cue interactions.

  - **Disconfirming outcome:** narrative condition alone explains essentially all variance.

- **RQ4 (Residual site effects; RIET-E entry criterion):** After cue-matching, blinding, and irritant control, is there any replicable residual target-site effect?

  - **Confirmatory support:** a robust residual site term that replicates across teams with locked analyses (supporting consideration of RIET-E).

  - **Disconfirming outcome:** residuals vanish with controls, favoring RIET-M only.

- **RQ5 (Mechanism probing; RIET-E):** If residuals exist, do they respond to sham-controlled physical interventions?

  - **Confirmatory support:** preregistered on/off or dose-response effects under effective masking.

  - **Disconfirming outcome:** null effects or non-replicable findings under sham control.

### 1.2 Assumptions, Scope, and Exclusions

RIET is designed as a scientific framework for *apparitional phenomenology*, not as proof of an afterlife. Its core commitment is methodological: ghost reports should be studied as structured perceptual events with measurable moderators and confounds. The framework is intentionally compatible with skeptical interpretations at the RIET-M level, while treating any RIET-E claims as conditional on rigorous residual effects under control.

- **In scope:** brief, involuntary, minimally interactive GPEs (presence, fragments, voices/impacts, somatic sensations) that are plausibly influenced by cue structure, state, and narrative priors.

- **Out of scope:** sustained interactive dialogues, mediumship claims, and any phenomena requiring intentional agency or complex information transfer; these are treated as separate classes requiring distinct models.

- **No survival assumption:** RIET does not require surviving consciousness to generate testable predictions; interpretations about spirits are treated as cultural appraisals of the experience rather than as required causal entities.

## 2.0 Core Mechanism

Residual Identity Echo Theory (RIET) models many ghost reports as the outcome of affective encoding plus later cue-driven reactivation.

This mechanism is grounded in known principles of emotional memory encoding, perceptual priming, and psychophysical feedback systems. The model proceeds through five interlinked phases, each supported by convergent evidence from neuroscience, environmental psychology, and psychophysics (Friston & Frith, 2015; Smith & Vela, 2001). These phases do not operate in strict linear sequence but form a dynamic, recursive cascade that can be initiated or amplified at multiple points.

### 2.1 Scope: Minimal vs. Extended RIET

RIET can be read at two complementary levels of commitment. The *minimal* account (RIET-M) explains Ghost Perception Events (GPEs) as context-triggered reactivations of affect-laden memory and identity representations within the observer, shaped by place cues, expectancy, and predictive processing. RIET-M makes no claim that information is literally stored outside the brain; instead, it treats “environmental resonance” as the ordinary ability of settings (odors, acoustics, layouts, lighting, symbols, and narratives) to cue and scaffold vivid simulation.

The *extended* account (RIET-E) adds the hypothesis that a subset of identity-linked signal structure may persist in the physical environment in ways that are not reducible to ordinary cueing alone (e.g., persistent field irregularities or material-state dependencies). RIET-E is evaluated only under the decision-gated criteria already specified (RQ4–RQ5; Section 5.3), and otherwise the framework reduces to RIET-M.

### 2.2 Operational Definitions and Falsifiability Commitments

**Identity echo (R):** a reproducible cluster of reported phenomenology (e.g., specific sensory fragments, affective tone, and spatial anchoring) that recurs across observers more often than expected by base-rate expectancy and generic fear responses.

- Cross-observer convergence on low-probability details

- Stable localization within a site (micro-location specificity)

- Consistent psychophysiological signatures time-locked to the experience (e.g., transient autonomic arousal)

**Environmental encoding (RIET-E claim):** any persistence of signal structure attributable to the site that remains when ordinary cues are minimized or controlled (e.g., narratives blinded, sensory features matched across comparison rooms, experimenters masked). Candidate RIET-E mechanisms must yield unique, preregistered predictions (e.g., on/off or dose-response effects under sham-controlled interventions) that distinguish them from cue-driven accounts.

**Observer convergence (P) and resonance:** the alignment between an observer’s internal state and the site’s trigger structure, indexed by factors such as arousal (sleep loss, stress), attentional set, absorption, and narrative framing.

**Activation alignment (A):** a latent propensity for a GPE operationalized as a composite of:

- Self-report intensity ratings

- Signal-detection performance under controlled tasks (e.g., false-alarm shifts) (Macmillan & Creelman, 2005)

- Psychophysiological change scores (EDA, HRV, EEG features) relative to baseline

**Threshold:** a pre-registered decision boundary (e.g., probability of report above control, or a multivariate classifier exceeding a set criterion) defining when a GPE is counted as present for analysis.

**Falsifiability commitments:** RIET-E is disfavored if the conditions below are met. Under those outcomes, RIET collapses to RIET-M: a neurocognitive, cue-driven account of GPEs. Conversely, RIET-E gains support only if robust, pre-registered, independently replicated site effects persist under blinding and cue-matching, and show systematic sensitivity to specific environmental manipulations with clear dose-response or on/off effects.

- Blinded, expectancy-controlled designs eliminate target-vs-control site differences.

- Matched control locations produce equivalent effects once ordinary cues are equated.

- Sham-controlled physical interventions (e.g., EMF shielding/modulation) show no replicable impact beyond placebo and measurement noise.

### 2.3 Identity Encoding Phase

RIET assumes that high-arousal, identity-salient episodes can be encoded with unusually strong affective and contextual bindings, increasing later reactivation when partial cues are encountered. This follows established work on emotional memory modulation and consolidation (Dolcos et al., 2005; McGaugh, 2015; Phelps, 2006; Sara, 2009).

In RIET-M, the “residual” is neurocognitive: stable cue constellations (odors, acoustics, textures, layout, symbols) repeatedly reinstate the same affective-memory network, increasing the chance of similar reconstructions across visits and observers (Brown & Kulik, 1977; Brewin, 2014; Knez, 2006; Smith & Vela, 2001).

**Note on proposed physical substrates (RIET-E):** Mechanistic proposals involving specific material carriers (e.g., microstructural changes in building materials, humidity-dependent effects, or other condensed-matter phenomena) should be treated as hypotheses to be *constrained by measurement*, not as established explanations. Accordingly, any candidate substrate must yield at least one unique, pre-registered prediction (e.g., sensitivity to shielding/modulation, time-constant decay functions, or spatial gradients) that distinguishes it from RIET-M’s cue-based account.

### 2.4 Environmental Priming

The surrounding environment functions as a *cue field* that can stabilize and amplify reactivation of identity-laden memory networks. In RIET-M, priming reflects ordinary mechanisms: repeated exposure to stable cues increases contextual reinstatement, while ambiguity increases reliance on top-down prediction and can yield agent-like percepts (Friston & Frith, 2015; Smith & Vela, 2001).

Practically, strong cueing plus uncertainty biases source monitoring: internally generated imagery or affect is more likely to be misattributed as external, yielding a “presence” or fragmentary apparition anchored to place cues (Johnson et al., 1993; Powers et al., 2016).

### 2.5 Observer Convergence

GPEs depend on *convergence*: a temporary alignment of cue ambiguity, observer state (e.g., arousal/fatigue), trait susceptibility (e.g., absorption), and interpretive priors. Under uncertainty, top-down priors can dominate perception and increase source-monitoring errors, producing presence-like and agent-like interpretations of ambiguous inputs (Arzy et al., 2006; Cheyne, 2001; Johnson et al., 1993; Powers et al., 2016).

Operationally, convergence can be modeled as the interaction of:

- **Trait variables:** absorption, fantasy proneness, dissociation, trauma history, and belief salience

- **State variables:** fatigue, acute stress, autonomic arousal

- **Cue structure:** darkness, reverberant acoustics, complex shadows, unfamiliar layouts, narrative framing

These factors can be measured using trait questionnaires, time-stamped state ratings and physiology, and standardized environmental metrics.

### 2.6 Echo Simulation

Once convergence is achieved, hippocampal pattern completion and predictive inference can reconstruct identity-linked fragments from partial cues. In RIET-M, GPEs are therefore expected to be brief, fragmented, and minimally interactive, consistent with biased source monitoring under uncertainty rather than intentional communication (Johnson et al., 1993; Kensinger & Schacter, 2006).

### 2.7 Echo Amplification

After a GPE, reconsolidation and social transmission can increase later reporting: repeated visits heighten attention to ambiguous cues, and retelling calibrates “ghost” priors. RIET-M predicts rising report rates with publicity and group attention even without any site-level persistence (Dunsmoor et al., 2015; McGaugh, 2015).

### 2.8 Boundary Conditions and Differentiators

- **Spatial specificity:** RIET predicts clustering in specific micro-locations (corners, thresholds, stairwells) that replicate across visits; generalized unease across an entire building is more consistent with irritants (CO/CO<sub>2</sub>, VOCs), infrasound, or broad expectancy (Wiseman et al., 2003).

- **Fragmentation and non-agency:** GPEs should be brief, loop-like, and minimally interactive; extended conversational entities would require a different model and should be treated as a separate phenomenon class.

- **State dependence:** susceptibility should increase with fatigue, stress, absorption, and heightened affect; however, if episodes occur primarily at sleep-wake boundaries with classic paralysis features, sleep-paralysis models are favored (Sharpless & Barber, 2011).

- **Expectancy sensitivity:** RIET-M predicts strong modulation by narrative priming; persistence interpretations require meeting the RIET-E Decision Gate.

- **Instrumental correlates:** spikes in reports that track ventilation/air-quality/flicker/infrasound favor environmental-irritant accounts; absence of covariation is only a prerequisite for (not evidence of) RIET-E.

### 2.9 Neurocognitive Implementation (Grounding in Systems Neuroscience)

RIET-M can be implemented in well-characterized neural systems without invoking nonstandard physics. First, **emotional tagging and durable encoding** are supported by amygdala–hippocampus interactions modulated by noradrenaline and glucocorticoids, which bias consolidation toward affectively salient material and strengthen context bindings (McGaugh, 2015; Sara, 2009). Second, **contextual reinstatement** is consistent with hippocampal pattern completion and the posterior medial episodic network, which can reconstruct scene context and “bring online” multimodal fragments when partial cues are encountered (Ranganath & Ritchey, 2012; Ritchey & Cooper, 2020).

Third, the common “presence” and “being watched” components of GPEs can be framed as **agency inference under uncertainty**. When sensory input is incomplete, brain systems involved in social perception and agency attribution (e.g., temporoparietal junction and associated midline networks) can be recruited to explain ambiguous cues, especially under threat or vigilance (Arzy et al., 2006; Cheyne, 2001). In parallel, the **salience network** (including anterior insula) can increase interoceptive gain, making bodily sensations (chills, pressure) more vivid and more likely to be interpreted as externally caused (Persinger, 2001).

Finally, state factors that increase anomalous perception (sleep loss, acute stress, novelty) plausibly operate via arousal and gain control (including locus coeruleus–noradrenaline signaling), increasing reliance on top-down inference (Sara, 2009). This helps explain why many GPEs are brief and fragmented: partial pattern completion yields a compelling but unstable reconstruction.

## 3.0 Internal Processing Dynamics

RIET is governed by a dynamic interplay between affectively encoded memory representations, contextual cue structure, and observer susceptibility. These interactions unfold recursively—forming a feedback loop that integrates encoding, inference under uncertainty, and phenomenological expression (Friston & Frith, 2015; McGaugh, 2015).

To formalize this, RIET introduces a symbolic model of identity echo activation (see Appendix A for complete symbol definitions): I(t₀) + E(t₀) → R(t₁) + P(t₁) → A(t₁) ≥ Threshold → GPE

This schematic captures the cascade: an original identity state (I) plus environmental configuration (E) yields a residual echo (R). When an observer’s perceptual configuration (P) aligns sufficiently, activation (A) crosses a preregistered threshold and a GPE occurs. The key implication is that effects should vary with time, cue stability, and observer state, and can be modeled with preregistered interactions.

Echo activation is expected when place cues, observer state (e.g., arousal, fatigue), and interpretive priors jointly increase top-down weighting and source-monitoring error probability. In practice, this is captured by the preregistered state×trait×cue interaction terms already specified under RIET-M.

### 3.1 Feedback and Echo Propagation

GPEs can propagate at the level of **people and narratives** even if no environmental persistence exists. In RIET-M, propagation occurs through:

- **Memory reconsolidation:** the experience is re-encoded and becomes easier to re-evoke on later visits.

- **Social transmission:** shared stories calibrate priors and increase attention to ambiguous cues.

- **Expectancy ecology:** changes in how a site is used, lit, staged, or toured can increase cue ambiguity and interpretive bias.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Empirically, propagation should appear as higher report rates after narrative exposure plus measurable criterion shifts on signal-detection tasks, with stronger group-level convergence when participants are not blinded.

### 3.2 Measurement Model: Operationalizing Activation Alignment

Treat activation alignment A(t) as a latent variable estimated from a small, preregistered set of observable channels (self-report, optional tasks, and physiology). Define a GPE using a locked decision rule relative to baseline and matched controls so RIET-M and RIET-E can be compared on the same measurement backbone.

### 3.3 Measurement Reliability and Rater Training

Because GPEs are partly subjective, reliability should be demonstrated (e.g., inter-rater agreement for Probable/Possible/Non-GPE classification). A full checklist, scoring rules, and rater-calibration guidance are provided in Appendix I.

### 3.4 Endpoint Definitions (Primary vs Secondary)

To limit false positives, define endpoints before collection. **Primary endpoints** should be few (e.g., Probable GPE rate per exposure window; mean intensity of Probable GPEs). **Secondary endpoints** can include physiology composites (EDA/HRV), criterion shifts on signal-detection tasks, and spatial clustering statistics. Environmental channels should be treated primarily as covariates/confound checks unless a preregistered RIET-E manipulation is being tested.

- **Self-report (primary):** time-stamped intensity rating, confidence, and a short structured phenomenology checklist (visual/auditory/somatic/presence; fragmentation; location within site).

- **Psychophysiology (secondary):** EDA peaks, HR/HRV change, respiration, and (if available) EEG features, all synchronized to report timestamps.

- **Environment (covariates):** CO/CO<sub>2</sub>, VOCs, temperature/humidity, illumination/flicker, acoustic/infrasound, and EMF bands, used to model and rule out confounding.

- **Signal-detection task (optional):** brief ambiguous-stimulus detection trials to quantify criterion shifts and false-alarm rates as a proxy for top-down weighting.

### 3.5 Alternative Explanations and Discriminating Tests

Because GPE reports can be influenced by multiple interacting factors, RIET is best evaluated by designs that explicitly discriminate among plausible mechanisms rather than by uncontrolled field observation alone. The most relevant alternatives include:

- **Expectation and suggestion:** demand characteristics and culturally learned scripts

- **Sleep-related and dissociative phenomena:** hypnagogia, sleep paralysis, depersonalization (Sharpless & Barber, 2011)

- **Environmental neuroirritants:** carbon monoxide/CO<sub>2</sub>, poor ventilation, flicker, infrasound (Tandy & Lawrence, 1998; Wiseman et al., 2003)

- **Predictive-coding under uncertainty:** ambiguous stimuli resolved into agent-like percepts (Friston & Frith, 2015)

#### 3.5.1 Negative Controls and Placebo Controls

To prevent over-interpreting base-rate anomalies, RIET studies should include explicit negative and placebo controls in addition to cue-matched comparisons. Recommended controls include:

- **Low-ambiguity room:** bright, acoustically simple, familiar layout (“boring” baseline)

- **Neutral narrative condition:** contains no ghost-relevant content

- **Sham interventions:** inactive shielding devices or placebo procedures with masking checks

#### 3.5.2 Competing-Model Evaluation (Pre-Specified)

To avoid post hoc explanation, RIET should be evaluated as a pre-specified set of competing (and, where applicable, nested) models fit to the same synchronized multimodal dataset. The minimum comparison set includes: (1) an **expectancy/demand** model (narrative condition + demand characteristics), (2) an **environmental-irritant** model (air quality, illumination/flicker, infrasound/acoustic proxies), (3) a **sleep–wake boundary** model (sleepiness, time-of-day/circadian proxies, paralysis/hypnagogic indicators), and (4) **RIET-M** (cue structure + state + traits + preregistered interactions). RIET-E enters model comparison only under the decision-gate criteria (RQ4–RQ5; Section 5.3). For a compact summary of discriminating signatures across these models, see Appendix C.

**Discriminating tests (illustrative designs):** A compact set of example study designs is provided in Appendix J.

### 3.6 Construct Validity: Defining and Distinguishing GPEs

A scientific framework for apparitional experiences must clearly define its target construct. RIET uses the term *Ghost Perception Event* (GPE) to denote a short-duration, involuntary perceptual episode with the following core features:

- **Spatially anchored:** tied to a location or micro-location

- **Phenomenologically fragmentary:** sensory snippets, presence, partial forms

- **Minimally interactive:** typically non-interactive or low-interaction

Construct validity improves when GPEs are separated from nearby phenomena that can look similar in casual reporting.

- **Sleep paralysis / hypnagogia:** tends to occur at sleep–wake boundaries, often with paralysis, chest pressure, and vivid entities; classify separately unless the episode is clearly site-anchored and occurs fully awake (Sharpless & Barber, 2011).

- **Panic and acute anxiety:** can produce derealization, chills, and threat interpretations; require concurrent measures of arousal and a check for panic symptoms to avoid misclassification.

- **Psychotic-spectrum hallucinations:** are typically less site-bound and may be chronic or accompanied by delusional interpretation; screening and exclusion criteria should be explicit in study protocols (Persinger, 2001).

- **Environmental intoxication/irritants:** CO/CO<sub>2</sub>, VOCs, and flicker can cause headaches, confusion, and perceptual anomalies; continuous monitoring and symptom logs are required to interpret field data.

- **Ordinary misperception:** momentary errors (pareidolia, mistaken shadows) are common; RIET requires either repeatable spatial anchoring, convergence across observers, or systematic links to measured cue structure (Johnson et al., 1993).

**RIET-GPE instrument (overview):** A short, standardized checklist plus a Probable/Possible/Non-GPE scoring rubric is recommended to reduce narrative inflation and improve cross-site comparability. The full checklist and scoring rules are provided in Appendix I.

### 3.7 Base Rates, Priors, and a Signal-Detection Framing

A rigorous ghost framework must treat anomalous reports against the background of nonzero base rates for misperception, pareidolia, presence feelings, and time-perception distortions—especially under darkness, novelty, fatigue, and social priming. RIET therefore recommends a signal-detection framing in which reports reflect both **sensitivity** to cue structure (how strongly the environment affords a particular percept) and **decision criterion** (how willing a person is to label an ambiguous experience as a “ghost”) (Macmillan & Creelman, 2005). Narrative priming is expected to shift criterion; cue-matching manipulations can affect sensitivity; irritants can affect both via physiological stress. Empirically, separating criterion shifts (false alarms) from sensitivity changes (d′) helps prevent over-interpreting increased report rates as evidence for site-specific effects.

### 3.8 Causal Structure (Confounds and Mediators)

Analyses should model cue structure, expectancy, and irritant covariates explicitly, and treat any residual site term as interpretable only after replication under masking (see Section 5.5 and Appendix H for protocol minimums).

## 4.0 Applications and Predictions

RIET generates preregisterable predictions suitable for field studies and controlled tests. Below, core predictions are stated with minimal design notes; extended protocol templates and checklists are provided in the appendices.

### 4.1 Spatial Specificity: Identity-Weighted Location Sensitivity and Micro-Location Heatmaps

**Prediction:** GPE reports will show replicable spatial structure: (a) higher incidence in identity-salient target locations than in cue-matched controls (IWLS), and (b) within-room micro-location clustering (“heatmaps”) above chance that replicates across independent samples.

**Operationalization:** IWLS can be indexed using documented event severity, exposure duration, and memorialization/social salience, alongside measured cue structure. Record within-room locations using a fixed scheme and quantify clustering with a preregistered statistic and permutation-based null.

**Test design:** Compare target rooms/sites to cue-matched controls; keep narratives blinded where feasible; preregister spatial resolution and clustering tests; replicate across sessions and (ideally) independent teams. Model cue metrics, expectancy, and state/trait moderators as covariates.

### 4.2 Trait Resonance Amplification

**Prediction:** Trait variables (e.g., absorption, dissociation, trait anxiety) will moderate GPE likelihood and intensity, with higher susceptibility under higher cue ambiguity.

**Operationalization:** Use validated trait scales (absorption/dissociation/anxiety) and preregister a trait-by-cue interaction model; treat “mirroring” as a quantitative interaction, not narrative matching (Swann, 1987).

**Test design:** Measure cue metrics (lighting/flicker, acoustics, layout ambiguity) and estimate trait×cue interactions in a preregistered hierarchical model; include negative-control rooms/narratives.

**Interpretation:** Trait×cue interactions are a core RIET-M prediction; any additional residual site/manipulation term is interpreted only via the RIET-E Decision Gate (Section 5.3).

### 4.3 Electromagnetic Field (EMF) Modulation

**Prediction:** At some locations, if electromagnetic conditions contribute causally to GPEs, then real (vs sham) EMF shielding/modulation will change preregistered GPE outcomes under effective blinding.

**Operationalization:** Primary outcomes: Probable GPE rate/intensity; secondary outcomes: EDA/HRV response and signal-detection criterion shifts (Macmillan & Creelman, 2005). Manipulation: randomized real vs sham shielding/modulation.

**Test design:** Double-blind where feasible; cue-matched control rooms; continuous monitoring of ventilation/CO/CO<sub>2</sub>, flicker, and infrasound; preregistered endpoints and masking checks (Wiseman et al., 2003).

**Interpretation:** A replicable real\>sham effect under effective masking is necessary (not sufficient) to motivate RIET-E; null effects favor RIET-M.

### 4.4 Secondary Cognitive Markers (Exploratory)

Exploratory secondary markers (e.g., brief time-perception distortions) may co-occur with higher-intensity GPEs via arousal/uncertainty mechanisms; if measured, they should be preregistered as secondary endpoints and interpreted cautiously (Droit-Volet & Meck, 2007).

### 4.5 Narrative Priming and Blinding Attenuation

**Prediction:** Narrative priming will shift reporting criterion, and blinding will reduce overall GPE rates. Under RIET-M, remaining variance should track measured cue structure and state×trait×cue interactions; under RIET-E, a residual target-site term should persist under verified blinding and cue-matching.

**Operationalization:** Conditions: accurate narrative vs plausible false narrative vs neutral/no narrative; outcomes: Probable GPE rate/intensity and criterion shifts (Macmillan & Creelman, 2005).

**Test design:** Factorial design crossing narrative condition with site type (target vs cue-matched control); script delivery and experimenter masking; manipulation checks for blinding integrity (Wiseman et al., 2003).

**Interpretation (RIET-M vs RIET-E):** Expectancy-only accounts predict narrative effects regardless of site; RIET-M predicts narrative×cue interactions; RIET-E predicts an additional residual target-site effect under effective blinding and cue-matching.

### 4.6 Echo Dissipation (Exploratory)

Exploratory prediction: GPE rates may decline following interventions that reduce uncertainty and narrative salience (e.g., memorialization or environmental changes), after controlling for cue shifts and expectancy exposure. An interrupted time-series with matched controls is the preferred design.

### 4.7 Analysis Plan Summary (Pre-Registered)

**Prediction:** Pre-registered hierarchical models will distinguish expectancy-only, irritant, sleep-boundary, and RIET-M accounts by quantifying variance explained and testing residual site terms under controls.

**Operationalization:** Primary endpoints: Probable GPE rate and intensity; secondary endpoints: physiology composite, criterion shifts, and spatial clustering; covariates: cue metrics and irritant measures. Analyses compare the pre-specified baseline model set (expectancy/demand, environmental irritants, sleepwake boundary, and RIET-M) using locked specifications.

**Test design:** Lock model specifications and robustness checks (irritant-spike exclusions; alternate GPE definitions; permutation tests for clustering). Use model comparison that penalizes complexity (e.g., out-of-sample predictive checks or information criteria), and report full specifications.

**Interpretation:** Interpret any residual site/manipulation term only after applying the RIET-E Decision Gate (Section 5.3).

## 5.0 Limitations and Future Work

This section summarizes known constraints on interpreting GPE reports, identifies threats to validity, and outlines the methodological and ethical requirements for future tests—especially the higher-bar RIET-E extension.

### 5.1 Threats to Validity (and Mitigations)

- **Construct validity:** misclassifying panic, sleep paralysis, or ordinary misperception as GPEs. *Mitigation:* RIET-GPE checklist + Probable/Possible rubric; concurrent state and symptom measures; irritant logging (Sharpless & Barber, 2011).

- **Internal validity:** demand characteristics, experimenter cues, and uncontrolled environmental differences between rooms. *Mitigation:* blinding integrity checks, scripted instructions, cue-matched controls, manipulation checks, and preregistered endpoints (Wiseman et al., 2003).

- **External validity:** effects that do not generalize beyond a specific site, population, or tourism context. *Mitigation:* multi-site replication, heterogeneous samples, and reporting of site context variables (visitation rate, staging).

- **Statistical conclusion validity:** inflated false positives due to many sensors and flexible analyses. *Mitigation:* limited primary endpoints, hierarchical models, correction/regularization strategies, and clear exploratory vs confirmatory separation.

### 5.2 Ethical and Legal Considerations for Site-Based Research

Field research at historically traumatic or privately owned locations introduces ethical and legal obligations beyond typical laboratory studies. Protocols should secure permission to collect sensor data, clarify photography/audio policies, protect the privacy of staff and visitors, and avoid sensational framing that could harm communities connected to the site’s history.

#### 5.2.1 Ethical Dissemination and Media Policy

- **Permissions and compliance:** obtain written access permissions and comply with site policies and local regulations for sensors, recordings, and participant activities.

- **Privacy and bystanders:** minimize collection of identifiable audio/video; post notices where required; anonymize any incidental data.

- **Respect for traumatic history:** engage with community stakeholders when appropriate; avoid language that trivializes harm; do not stage provocations in memorial spaces.

- **Participant wellbeing:** include clear withdrawal procedures and post-session debriefing; provide support resources when distress is triggered.

### 5.3 Limitations of RIET-E (Extended Model)

RIET-E is framed as a high-bar extension because site-level “persistence” hypotheses are difficult to identify uniquely in noisy field settings. Apparent residuals can be produced by subtle unmeasured cue differences, incomplete blinding, or complex interactions among state, traits, and context; accordingly, RIET-E should be evaluated only after RIET-M and competing explanations are thoroughly stress-tested.

- **Identifiability and underspecification:** many different site variables can mimic a “residual” term. *Safeguard:* strict cue-matching documentation, negative-control rooms, and hierarchical models that explicitly include measured cue covariates.

- **Measurement non-specificity:** common candidate signals (broadband EMF, acoustics, microclimate) are not uniquely diagnostic of any persistence mechanism. *Safeguard:* require RIET-E hypotheses to generate unique, preregistered signatures (spatial gradients, decay constants, on/off effects) rather than relying on correlational “anomalies.”

- **Multiple comparisons and analytic flexibility:** many sensors and outcomes increase false positives. *Safeguard:* limited primary endpoints, preregistered thresholds, and correction/regularization plus full reporting of all tested channels.

- **Intervention interpretability:** a “positive” physical intervention effect can reflect placebo, expectation leakage, or unintended cue changes (noise, temperature, lighting). *Safeguard:* sham control, masking verification, manipulation checks, and logging of collateral environmental changes during intervention.

- **Generalizability:** even a replicable effect at one site may not generalize. *Safeguard:* multi-site replication with independent teams and shared locked protocols before strong claims.

- **Post hoc theorizing risk:** persistence stories can proliferate after the fact. *Safeguard:* RIET-E entry criteria (RQ4/RQ5), registered reports for confirmatory stages, and explicit stopping rules when residuals fail to replicate.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

**RIET-E Decision Gate (Required Before Mechanism Claims)**

- **Gate 1 (Residual required):** Do not interpret any “persistence” mechanism unless a residual target-site effect replicates under cue-matching and verified blinding (RQ4).

- **Gate 2 (Sham control required):** Do not interpret intervention effects unless real vs sham procedures are masked and manipulation checks confirm masking integrity (RQ5).

- **Gate 3 (Negative controls required):** Include negative-control rooms/narratives and demonstrate that effects are not global arousal or tourism-context artifacts.

- **Gate 4 (Independent replication required):** Treat single-team findings as provisional; require independent-team replication with a locked analysis plan.

Key practical constraints include reliance on subjective reports, imperfect instrumentation for subtle site variables, and cultural contamination of interpretation. These limits increase the importance of blinding checks, cue-matching documentation, and independent replication.

**Participant safety:** Use clear withdrawal/stopping rules, distress checks, and debriefing, especially at trauma-linked sites; avoid sensational framing and follow site/community guidance.

Future directions include:

- Biometric co-tracking during field experiments, pairing EEG, heart-rate, and skin-conductance measures with real-time environmental sensors.

- Cross-cultural GPE studies to assess universality versus cultural modulation of echo expression.

- Development of clear diagnostic criteria for residual identity echoes, potentially including standardized phenomenological scales.

- Controlled laboratory analogs using virtual reality and emotionally charged priming to simulate echo formation under ethical conditions.

### 5.4 Empirical Roadmap: From Exploratory to Confirmatory Tests

RIET research is best approached as staged validation: first map base rates and spatial structure, then attenuate expectancy and control cues, then replicate across teams. Only if a residual persists under those controls should sham-controlled mechanism probes be attempted.

- **Stage 1 (Exploratory mapping):** instrumented site surveys to identify candidate micro-locations and to quantify base rates under neutral framing. Output: location heatmaps, sensor baselines, and candidate confounds.

- **Stage 2 (Cue control and expectancy attenuation):** cue-matched control rooms, standardized lighting/acoustics, and double-blind narrative conditions. Output: preregistered estimates of narrative effects, cue effects, and their interaction.

- **Stage 3 (Confirmatory replication):** multi-site replication with shared protocols, independent teams, and locked analysis plans. Output: effect sizes with confidence intervals, not just significance testing.

- **Stage 4 (Mechanism probing):** only if residual site effects survive Stage 2–3, introduce sham-controlled physical interventions (e.g., EMF shielding/modulation) with rigorous masking and manipulation checks.

- **Stage 5 (Model comparison):** evaluate RIET-M vs RIET-E using model-selection approaches (e.g., pre-registered hierarchical models that include trait variables, narrative condition, cue metrics, and environmental covariates) to quantify how much variance is explained by each component.

Across stages, prioritize replication, effect sizes/precision, and robustness to alternative explanations, with standardized instruments and verified blinding checks.

Adversarial collaborations and multi-team replications can further reduce interpretive drift by locking protocols, analyses, and decision rules in advance.

### 5.5 Field Protocol (Brief) and Reporting Minimums

To keep RIET empirically testable without over-specifying a single implementation, the minimum publishable field protocol can be stated compactly as follows (details may be supplied in appendices or a companion protocol document):

- Blinding: scripted instructions; experimenter masking where feasible; post-room manipulation checks (guess of condition/history + confidence) reported.

- Cue control: cue-matched control rooms/sites with documented equivalence on illumination/flicker, acoustics, temperature/humidity, and layout salience.

- Event capture: time-stamped reports plus a short GPE checklist; standardized micro-location logging to enable heatmaps.

- Confounds: continuous CO/CO<sub>2</sub>/VOCs and other irritant proxies logged and modeled as covariates; prespecified rules for sensor failure and artifact handling.

- Analysis discipline: preregistered primary endpoints, thresholds, and model-comparison plan; apply the RIET-E Decision Gate (Section 5.3) for any persistence/mechanism claims.

- Reporting minimums: narrative scripts, blinding success rates, cue-matching criteria, sensor specifications/time synchronization, and full null reporting.

Expanded protocol details (sensors, exclusions, sampling, study flow, spatial logging, and adverse-event procedures) are provided in Appendix H; recommended companion materials include the site characterization minimum fields (Appendix D), the core data dictionary/variable list (Appendix E), and the glossary of key terms (Appendix F).

### 5.6 Laboratory Analogs (Ethical, Repeatable Paradigms)

To reduce dependence on rare “haunted” sites and to improve replicability, RIET can be tested using controlled laboratory analogs that preserve the essential ingredients: contextual cueing, uncertainty, narrative priors, and state modulation. These paradigms do not prove (or disprove) RIET-E, but they can strongly validate RIET-M mechanisms and generate calibrated effect sizes for field studies.

- **VR contextual reinstatement:** create two visually similar VR “rooms” with controlled lighting/acoustics. Pair one room with an emotionally salient narrative and memory task (ethical, non-traumatic), then test whether later re-exposure increases presence reports and criterion shifts relative to a cue-matched control room under blinded narratives.

- **Ambiguous stimulus detection with priors:** present ambiguous audio/visual stimuli embedded in “location” contexts (background scenes) while manipulating priors (“this site is associated with X”). Use signal detection metrics (d′, criterion) to quantify how priors and cue structure jointly shape false alarms and perceived presence.

- **Social transmission and expectancy ecology:** compare solo vs group conditions and vary whether participants hear prior participants’ “stories.” Test whether micro-location clustering emerges from shared attention guidance even when the physical environment is constant.

### 5.7 Open-Science Defaults (Brief)

- Preregister confirmatory questions, primary endpoints, thresholds, and the analysis plan before data collection (see Appendix G for templates and governance notes).

- Share scripts, cue-matching criteria, and a minimal data dictionary sufficient for reanalysis.

- Report manipulation-check outcomes (blinding integrity) and null results with the same detail as positive findings.

- Keep RIET-E claims contingent on independently replicated residual effects under masking and cue matching.

## 6.0 Final Synthesis

Residual Identity Echo Theory offers a mechanistically grounded account of how intense identity-linked states can be encoded in memory and later reactivated as apparitional experiences when observers encounter the right configuration of cues, states, and priors.

RIET’s contribution is to treat ghost reports as analyzable data: structured phenomenology plus synchronized environmental and physiological measurement, evaluated through preregistered discriminating tests and explicit model comparison.

In summary, RIET’s core contributions are:

- Defines a standardized target construct (GPE) with scoring rules and measurement expectations.

- Grounds apparitional phenomenology in established memory and inference mechanisms (RIET-M).

- Specifies a high-bar pathway for evaluating any site-level persistence claims (RIET-E) via preregistered, blinded, cue-matched, and replicated tests.

## Appendix F: Glossary of Key Terms

- **Ghost Perception Event (GPE):** a brief, involuntary, typically minimally interactive perceptual episode interpreted as a ghost encounter.

- **Identity echo:** a reproducible cluster of phenomenology that recurs across observers and/or visits beyond base-rate expectation.

- **Cue field:** the stable constellation of environmental cues (lighting, acoustics, odors, layout) that can trigger contextual reinstatement and predictive inference.

- **Convergence:** the alignment of state, traits, cue structure, and priors that increases the likelihood of a GPE.

- **Cue-matching:** selecting control rooms/sites matched on relevant sensory features to isolate narrative/history effects from environment.

- **Residual site effect:** a target-site term that remains after modeling narrative, cues, traits, state, and confounds; prerequisite for considering RIET-E.

## Appendix H: Field Protocol Details (Expanded)

### H.1 Sensor Calibration, Sampling, and Time Synchronization

Field inference depends on measurement quality. Protocols should specify: sensor models, sampling rates, calibration procedures (pre/post session and periodic reference checks), and a shared time base (e.g., all devices synchronized to the same clock with documented drift). Record raw data whenever possible, preserve metadata, and define how missingness is handled (interpolation rules vs session-level exclusion). For flicker, specify whether measurement is photodiode-based or derived from video, and report limitations (Wiseman et al., 2003).

### H.2 Data Exclusion, Artifact Handling, and Sensitivity Analyses

Pre-register rules for excluding or flagging data to prevent cherry-picking. Examples: exclude sessions with sensor failure beyond a defined threshold, acute intoxication, or medical distress; flag (rather than remove) events reported without timestamps; and model results both with and without events coincident with irritant spikes. Report sensitivity analyses that show whether conclusions depend on particular thresholds or scoring choices.

### H.3 Reporting Checklist (Minimum for Publishable RIET Studies)

- Exact narrative scripts and blinding procedures (plus blinding success rates and manipulation-check results).

- Cue-matching criteria and measured room/site equivalence (illumination, flicker, acoustics, temperature/humidity, layout metrics).

- Sensor models, sampling rates, calibration schedule, and time synchronization method (plus sensor QC metrics and missingness handling).

- Primary endpoints, scoring rubric, preregistered thresholds, and exclusion/flagging rules.

- Full reporting of null results, robustness checks, and sensitivity analyses.

- Spatial logging method for micro-location heatmaps and the clustering statistic/test used (e.g., permutation test).

### H.4 Sampling, Recruitment, and Screening

Sampling choices can dominate results in anomalous-perception studies. To improve external validity, report recruitment sources (tour groups vs general population), target a balance of belief levels where feasible, and measure belief salience/expectancy explicitly at baseline (e.g., short ratings of “how likely is a ghost experience here?” and “how much do you expect to have an experience today?”). Screening should assess sleep disorders (including frequent sleep paralysis), acute intoxication, and vulnerability to distress; protocols should also specify how psychotic-spectrum risk is handled (exclusion vs separate observational arm). When sample sizes permit, stratify or block-randomize by key traits (absorption, dissociation) so conditions remain comparable.

### H.5 Effect Sizes, Precision Goals, and Decision Thresholds

Beyond statistical significance, RIET studies should target interpretable effect sizes and precision. Define a smallest effect size of interest (SESOI) for primary endpoints (e.g., a practically meaningful increase in Probable GPE rate relative to cue-matched control), and plan sampling to achieve useful confidence-interval widths for those effects. For transparency, preregister example decision thresholds such as: a minimum intensity cutoff for Probable GPE classification (e.g., ≥ a defined scale point), and a minimum clustering effect criterion (e.g., permutation-tested clustering plus a minimum cluster compactness statistic). These thresholds should be set before data are examined.

### H.6 Study Flow Overview (Textual)

1.  Consent, screening, and baseline measures (traits, belief/expectancy, sleepiness/stress).

2.  Baseline acclimation period; sensor calibration check; device time sync verification.

3.  Randomization to room order and narrative condition; delivery of scripted instructions.

4.  Standardized exposure window with time-stamped event logging (button press) and continuous sensors.

5.  Immediate RIET-GPE checklist completion for each event; brief post-room manipulation checks (blinding integrity).

6.  Post-session tasks (optional): time perception and/or ambiguous-stimulus detection to estimate criterion shifts.

7.  Debriefing, adverse-event check, and documentation of any protocol deviations.

8.  Locked analysis pipeline execution; model comparison; robustness checks and sensitivity analyses.

### H.7 Micro-Location Logging Standard

Because spatial specificity is a core discriminator (RQ2), record locations using a consistent within-room reference system. At minimum, define the room boundary, mark fixed anchors (doorways, windows), and record locations using:

- Named zones (e.g., “NW corner,” “stair landing”)

- Approximate coordinates on a simple grid or floorplan when feasible

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Report the spatial resolution used and apply the same scheme across target and control rooms.

### H.8 Adverse Events, Distress Monitoring, and Stopping Rules

Protocols should include an adverse-event definition (e.g., panic attacks, dissociative episodes, medically concerning symptoms) and a standardized distress check after each exposure. Predefine stopping rules (participant-level and study-level) and document all adverse events, including whether they co-occurred with environmental irritant spikes. This both protects participants and prevents confounding intense distress with purported “high-intensity” GPEs.

## Appendix I: RIET-GPE Checklist and Scoring Rubric

Proposed minimum phenomenology instrument (RIET-GPE checklist):

1.  Modality: visual / auditory / somatic / presence

2.  Duration (seconds) and onset/offset abruptness

3.  Interactivity: none / low / high

4.  Spatial anchoring: room + within-room location

5.  Fragmentation: single cue vs multi-cue scene

6.  Appraisal: threat / grief / neutrality

7.  Confidence and alternative interpretations

8.  Concurrent state: sleepiness, stress, intoxication symptoms

9.  Immediate environmental notes (e.g., lighting changes, drafts, noises)

Using a standardized checklist reduces narrative inflation and improves cross-site comparability.

**Scoring rubric (recommended):** To increase reliability across studies, classify events as Probable GPE, Possible GPE, or Non-GPE using preregistered thresholds.

**Probable GPE requires:**

- Clear spatial anchoring to a room and within-room micro-location

- Brief duration (seconds to a few minutes) with abrupt or clearly demarcated onset

- Low interactivity (none/low)

- At least one sensory or presence feature rated above a preregistered intensity threshold

**Possible GPE:** Spatial anchoring is present but duration and/or interactivity criteria are ambiguous, or intensity is below the Probable threshold.

**Non-GPE:** Primary features align with panic/sleep-paralysis markers or the event coincides with clear irritant spikes (e.g., CO/CO<sub>2</sub>, VOC, flicker) per preregistered exclusion/flagging rules.

## Appendix J: Discriminating Test Designs (Illustrative)

1.  **Double-blind narrative priming with matched/mismatched histories:** participants receive either an accurate site narrative, a plausible but false narrative, or no narrative; investigators interacting with participants are masked to condition. Outcome: GPE rate/intensity and psychophysiology.

2.  **Cue-matched control locations:** compare a target room to a control room matched on lighting, acoustics, temperature, layout, and visual salience (ideally within the same building). Outcome: whether effects remain when ordinary cues are equated.

3.  **Environmental monitoring to rule out irritants:** continuous CO/CO<sub>2</sub>, VOCs, temperature gradients, humidity, illumination/flicker, and infrasound logging synchronized with reports. Outcome: determine whether events cluster around physiological stressors.

4.  **Sham-controlled EMF shielding/modulation:** apply real vs sham shielding or field modulation with randomization and masking; analyze preregistered endpoints for on/off or dose-response effects.

5.  **Pre-registration and signal-detection framing:** treat GPE reports as detection decisions under uncertainty; quantify false alarms and criterion shifts to separate decision criterion shifts from sensitivity changes.
