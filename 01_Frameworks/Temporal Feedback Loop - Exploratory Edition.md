### Abstract

The continuity of conscious experience across time is one of the deepest open problems in the study of mind. The Temporal Feedback Loop (TFL) proposes that this continuity is not given, but generated — produced moment to moment by a recursive process that integrates current sensory input, reactivated memory, and a content-identifiable predictive simulation into a single integrated state, cycle after cycle, for as long as the system remains active. Affective intensity is registered on the same cycle but is not one of the ingredients of that integration; instead, it separately determines how strongly the resulting representation will persist, recur, and resist dissolution once integration has already fixed what it contains. A simulation that carries enough affective weight does not simply appear and vanish; it becomes an open loop, propagating forward through successive cycles until it is closed by action, by confirmation from the world, or by the quiet erosion of its own affective charge.

TFL treats this recursive integration as the generative substrate of felt experience itself. What is experienced as "the present moment" is not a passive readout of the senses; it is the output of a synthesis of sensory evidence, memory-based expectation, and the currently active simulation — recomputed every cycle and carried forward as the seed of the next, with affective intensity determining how long the result survives. Belief is not a separate faculty sitting apart from perception; it is a stabilized, affectively reinforced structural prior operating across cycles rather than within any single one of them, durable enough to shape what memories are retrieved and what futures are treated as plausible when input is ambiguous.

The mechanism is built from five variables, one integration function, one persistence rule, and one structural prior. From that single architecture, the full range of phenomena described in this edition follows directly. Loops resolve through the agent's own action, through environmental confirmation, or through affectively recognized observation of an equivalent event. Repeated, affectively supported self-referential simulation converges into identity. Loops in different individuals become partially coupled as each person's behavior functions as input for others. States in which contradiction-checking input is reduced shift the loop's balance toward simulation-dominated content, producing altered phenomenology. Persistent thought, belief stabilization, delayed motivation, identity, social synchronization, and altered states are all expressions of the same recursive loop operating under different conditions.

### 1.0 Introduction

Why does experience feel continuous when its underlying substrate — neural firing, sensory sampling, attentional allocation — is anything but continuous? Why do certain thoughts return uninvited, hours or days after they seemed to have passed? Why does a belief, once formed, begin to reshape what a person notices, remembers, and expects, in a way that makes the belief harder to dislodge each time it is confirmed? Why does a person come to experience themselves as a stable self, and why do groups of people drift toward shared expectations and shared moods? These are not separate puzzles. TFL proposes that they are surface expressions of one recursive mechanism.

The core intuition is this: cognition does not process information once and discard it. It recycles. Sensory input, retrieved memory, and a content-identifiable predictive simulation are integrated into a working representation of the present moment, and that representation does not disappear once the moment has passed — it becomes the prior for the next cycle. Whether it survives into that next cycle at all is governed separately by how much affective charge it carries: enough charge, and it resurfaces, biases downstream interpretation of ambiguous input toward itself, and pulls behavior toward outcomes that would confirm it; too little, and it decays quickly, leaving almost no trace. The felt sense of a continuous, coherent stream of experience is what this recursive carry-over feels like from the inside.

TFL's founding commitments are as follows. First, affective intensity is the variable that determines whether a given simulation survives from one cycle to the next — it is the gain control on persistence; it does not decide what the simulation contains. Second, belief operates as a structural, affectively stabilized prior that reaches backward into memory retrieval and forward into simulation content, constraining both toward self-confirmation when the incoming input is ambiguous. Third, contradiction is what redirects an already-active loop: as contradicting evidence accumulates against a persistent simulation, the loop is pushed toward either revising that simulation's content or suppressing its recurrence outright. Fourth, the loop is not confined to a single timescale, a single individual, or a single state of consciousness — the same architecture produces moment-to-moment continuity, long-term identity, interpersonal synchronization, and altered-state experience. Fifth, none of this requires positing hidden machinery beyond the interaction of sensory input, memory, affect, and simulation, and the framework earns its explanatory value precisely where it outperforms simpler accounts built only from uncertainty, generic arousal, or habituation.

### 2.0 Core Mechanism

#### 2.1 Memory Reactivation as the Engine of Context

The hippocampus does not store static records; it reconstructs. Every act of remembering is closer to reconstruction than replay, drawing on distributed traces and reassembling them in light of the present moment's demands. Within TFL, this reconstructive capacity is what supplies Minfo(t) — the contextually relevant memory content that grounds a given cycle's simulation in something more than raw sensory input. Compressed, accelerated reactivation of prior sequences during quiet wakefulness and rest is the substrate through which simulations gain their specific, situationally appropriate content rather than remaining generic anticipations. The more precisely a memory trace can be reactivated, the more precisely the resulting simulation can be shaped — which is why emotionally significant memories, encoded with unusual vividness, generate simulations that feel unusually concrete and compelling.

#### 2.2 Integration as the Site of Synthesis

Sensory evidence, reactivated memory, and the active predictive simulation are brought together into one workable representation — a synthesis rather than a mere concatenation. TFL locates this synthesis function at the point where distributed cortical and subcortical signals converge into a single integrated state, denoted F(t). This integrative site does not merely aggregate; it assigns relative weight to Input(t), Minfo(t), and Sim(t), resolves conflicts between what is expected and what is sensed, and produces the representation that will be carried forward as the next cycle's starting prior. Affective intensity is not one of the integration function's arguments — it is registered alongside integration, on the same cycle, but it does not enter the weighting that produces F(t) and does not supply representational content to it. Integration decides what the moment contains; affective intensity decides, separately, how long that content survives.

#### 2.3 Affective Salience as Gain Control on Persistence

Affective intensity — denoted E(t) — is the variable that determines how loudly a given cycle's content will echo forward. It governs persistence. It does not enter the integration that produces F(t), and it does not determine which simulation is generated or what that simulation predicts — those are settled by sensory evidence, reactivated memory, and the belief-based prior described below. High-salience content is more likely to survive from one cycle into the next, more readily reactivated after a quiet interval, and more resistant to extinction than low-salience content carrying the same informational payload. A simulation with negligible affective charge is integrated, produces its momentary effect, and is gone. A simulation with substantial affective charge becomes an attractor — it recurs, it resists competing input, and it can remain latent for extended periods before reactivating under conditions of contextual overlap, fatigue, or attentional drift. In every case, what recurs is content that was already fixed by integration, not content that affect itself invented.

#### 2.4 Simulation as Forward-Directed Construction

The system does not wait passively for the future to arrive; it constructs forward models continuously, using memory and current context as raw material. Within TFL this is formalized as Sim(t) — a predictive representation shaped by Input(t), Minfo(t), and the prior integrated state F(t-1), and fed back into the next cycle's integration. Affective intensity does not shape what Sim(t) anticipates; it governs whether that anticipation, once generated, is carried forward. The same architecture that allows a person to remember a past afternoon allows them to pre-experience an afternoon that has not yet happened, which is why imagined futures and remembered pasts share so much of their phenomenal texture. Simulation is not a special add-on faculty but the same reconstructive process as memory, pointed forward instead of backward. These simulations persist recursively — they do not resolve automatically simply because time has passed. They wait.

#### 2.5 Belief as a Structural Prior

Belief is not a momentary signal computed fresh on every cycle; it is a stabilized, affectively reinforced constraint that operates across cycles rather than within any single one of them. Once a belief has become durable enough, it biases which memories are reinstated as Minfo(t) and which possible simulations are treated as plausible when Sim(t) is generated under ambiguous input. Belief does not add a sixth variable to the integration function — it conditions how the existing variables are populated, in the same way a lens does not appear inside a photograph but determines what the photograph can show. This is why two systems facing the same ambiguous input integrate that input into markedly different simulations.

#### 2.6 A Substrate-Independent Architecture

The mechanism described in Sections 2.1 through 2.5 is not restricted to a single substrate. Any system capable of integrating time-indexed input, reactivated memory, and a content-identifiable predictive simulation, with a separate affect-analogous signal governing persistence and a structural prior conditioning retrieval under ambiguity, exhibits the same qualitative dynamics — persistence, escalation, delayed reentry, and contradiction-driven revision or suppression — whether that system is biological or artificial. The same architecture also operates across states of consciousness: dreaming, deep absorption, and imagination are conditions in which the loop runs with its ordinary contradiction-checking input loosened, as described in Section 4.7.

### 3.0 Internal Processing Dynamics

#### 3.1 Formal Components of the Loop

The loop operates over discrete temporal windows and is defined by five interacting variables, restated here with their canonical roles for direct reference:

| Symbol | Definition | Role |
|---|---|---|
| `Input(t)` | Sensory information available at the current moment | Integration input |
| `Minfo(t)` | Memory content reactivated and made relevant to the current moment | Integration input |
| `Sim(t)` | Predictive simulation constructed from Input(t), Minfo(t), and the prior integrated state | Integration input |
| `E(t)` | Affective-autonomic intensity at the current moment | Persistence control — not an integration input |
| `F(t)` | Fully integrated representation of the current cycle, carried forward as the next cycle's prior | Output of integration |
| Belief prior | Affectively stabilized structural constraint on retrieval and simulation under ambiguity | Cross-cycle constraint, not a momentary variable |

Of these five, only Input(t), Minfo(t), and Sim(t) enter the integration function. E(t) is registered on the same cycle but is not an argument of that function:

```text
F(t) = Integration[Input(t), Minfo(t), Sim(t)]
```

```text
Persistence of Sim(t) into the next cycle depends on E(t)
```

Integration here denotes the synthesis of perceptual input and memory-based expectation with the currently active simulation into one workable present-moment state, which then seeds retrieval and simulation in the following cycle. A belief-based prior constrains this process across cycles without being represented as an additional momentary function of E(t).

#### 3.2 The Four Phases of a Loop Cycle

**Encoding.** A stimulus, thought, or belief enters the system. If it arrives with elevated affective charge, it is preferentially encoded, and the magnitude of that charge governs the probability of later reactivation.

**Integration.** The encoded content is fused with reactivated memory and current sensory input, producing a forward simulation that may operate beneath conscious notice or may be explicitly imagined.

**Propagation.** If the simulation remains unresolved, it continues to circulate. It may intensify, adapt, or lie dormant depending on ongoing input and internal shifts, and it does so across timescales ranging from sub-second micro-loops to reactivation that surfaces only after days or longer.

**Resolution.** The loop closes when the simulation is behaviorally enacted, when the environment confirms it — directly or through an observed equivalent event — when its affective charge decays below the threshold needed to sustain it, or when accumulating contradiction pushes the loop toward revision or suppression. Once resolved, the representation is discharged, attention reallocates, and the system is available for new cycles.

#### 3.3 Contradiction: Revision Versus Suppression

Disconfirmation is not a single event but a matter of degree, and the loop responds to it in one of two structurally distinct ways. In **revision**, the active simulation's content changes to better accommodate the contradicting evidence while the loop itself remains open — the anticipated outcome is edited rather than abandoned. In **suppression**, the active simulation stops recurring without being replaced by a revised version — the loop closes not because it was satisfied but because it was overridden. Which of the two occurs depends jointly on how much affective support the simulation still carries and how strongly the belief prior favors reinterpretation over abandonment: well-supported, belief-congruent simulations tend toward revision under mild contradiction, while weakly supported ones tend toward outright suppression under the same pressure. This distinction is essential, because a system that only ever suppressed under contradiction, never revised, would not need a simulation-generating mechanism at all — a much simpler on/off gate would do the same work.

#### 3.4 Stability, Escalation, and Resolution

A loop is stable when its content recurs across cycles without meaningful change — the same anticipated outcome, reasserting itself with roughly constant force. A loop escalates when recurrence, affective intensity, or behavioral pull increases across successive cycles, typically because each partial confirmation feeds back into the belief structure that generated the simulation in the first place, deepening the attractor. A loop resolves when one of the closure conditions above is met, at which point the affective charge that sustained it is free to dissipate.

For direct reference, the conditions governing continuation versus resolution are as follows. **Continuation conditions** — a loop persists into further cycles when affective intensity remains elevated, when the active simulation remains unresolved or is re-triggered by context, and when reactivated memory continues to reinforce a congruent simulation. **Resolution conditions** — a loop closes when the simulation is behaviorally enacted, when it is externally confirmed through matched sensory input or an affectively recognized equivalent event, when affective intensity decays below the level needed to sustain it, or when cognitive reinterpretation dissolves the simulation's original force.

#### 3.5 Delayed Reentry

A loop need not resolve quickly, and it need not announce its return. Content that has gone quiet reactivates later — sometimes much later — when context overlaps sufficiently with the conditions of original encoding, or when affective readiness happens to align. This is why a thought that seemed to pass resurfaces as unplanned motivation, and why unresolved emotional material reasserts itself years after the event that generated it, triggered by nothing more than an incidental cue.

#### 3.6 Variable Closure and Boundary Conditions

TFL operates wherever predictive content remains distinguishable, affective intensity governs persistence, and contradiction can alter the course of the loop. It is built entirely from the declared variables of sensory input, retrieved memory content, predictive simulation, affective intensity, the integrated current state, and belief-based prior constraint. The balance between these variables shifts across conditions — most notably, the availability of contradiction-checking sensory input varies between ordinary waking perception and states such as dreaming or absorption — and these shifts account for the differing character of experience across states. Under chaotic input, in the absence of any identifiable simulation content, or under extreme physiological disruption, the loop's dynamics degrade, and no additional undeclared variables are introduced to cover such cases.

#### 3.7 Distinguishing the Loop from Simpler Accounts

A system that simply carries generic uncertainty forward, a system that is merely aroused without any content-specific memory, and a system that is only repeating a habituated response can each mimic persistence, bias, and even something resembling reentry without any recursive simulation doing the work. TFL is distinguished from these accounts by the fact that recurrence, ambiguity-driven bias, delayed reentry, and contradiction-driven revision or suppression remain tied to identifiable simulation content and to affective intensity that is not reducible to sensory uncertainty or nonspecific arousal. Wherever these phenomena are fully explained by uncertainty alone, by arousal alone, or by habit alone, the loop architecture is not the operative explanation in that instance.

### 4.0 Applications and Predictions

#### 4.1 Persistent Thought and Simulation Reentry

Thoughts, intentions, and hypothetical narratives are simulations like any other, and their fate is governed by the same affective gain control as everything else in the loop. Low-salience simulations decay quickly and leave little trace. High-salience simulations persist, bias attention and memory retrieval toward themselves, alter the interpretation of ambiguous input in their own favor, and lie latent as priors capable of reasserting themselves at any later point where context permits. An unresolved emotional charge is an open loop — something the system keeps circling back to until it is closed.

#### 4.2 Belief as a Self-Confirming Structural Encoding

Belief is not a detached cognitive judgment; it is an affectively stabilized prior woven into the loop's ongoing operation. Once a belief has been reinforced enough times with sufficient affective backing, it biases memory retrieval toward confirmatory content, constrains simulated futures toward belief-consistent outcomes, and filters ambiguous incoming information so that confirmatory readings are favored. This produces a recursive, self-confirming dynamic: belief shapes perception, and perception reaffirms belief, cycle after cycle, until belief-congruent simulations come to dominate the loop's ongoing content almost entirely, regardless of how proportionate that dominance is to the evidence actually available.

#### 4.3 Identity Convergence

Identity is the convergence of recursively reinforced self-referential simulation. When a self-referential simulation — "I am reliable," "I am always overlooked" — is generated repeatedly, receives repeated affective support, and is repeatedly closed by behavior or feedback that confirms rather than contradicts it, the persistence-and-reentry dynamics of the loop make that content recur far more reliably than an ordinary simulation. Behavior aligns to the role, the environment responds to the behavior, and that response closes the loop in the role's favor, further stabilizing it. Over time, transient self-referential simulations become durable components of self-concept. The self is not a fixed entity standing behind the loop; it is the loop's most persistently reinforced content, continuously re-integrated across time.

#### 4.4 Distributed and Observational Resolution

Loop resolution does not require direct action. Environmental confirmation need not be confirmation of the agent's own situation: when an observed, described, or imagined event is affectively recognized as equivalent to an active simulation's anticipated outcome, it satisfies the same confirmation role that direct experience would otherwise fill. A simulation of conflict resolves when the agent witnesses an equivalent conflict elsewhere and affectively registers the match. Direct participation and observational confirmation are therefore alternative pathways to the same closure. This predicts that vicarious exposure to an equivalent event measurably reduces recurrence of a matched simulation, in a manner distinguishable from mere forgetting or distraction.

#### 4.5 Escalating and Intrusive Loops

When none of the resolution conditions are met — no enactment, no confirmation, no affective decay, no reinterpretation — the simulation continues to recur rather than resolving on its own. When the surrounding context repeatedly supplies partial or ambiguous input that neither confirms nor clearly contradicts the active simulation, each unresolved cycle reinforces rather than weakens affective support for it. Intrusive and recurring thought is therefore sustained recursive propagation — the ordinary persistence mechanism operating without an available closure condition — rather than a separate psychological process.

#### 4.6 Social Synchronization

Humans continuously function as environmental input for one another. Because observed events can satisfy or contradict an observer's simulations, the same mechanism operates in multiple directions at once within a group: when several individuals hold affectively similar unresolved simulations, each individual's behavior, language, expression, and attention becomes confirming or contradicting input for the others, and their loops become partially coupled. Shared environments therefore produce partial synchronization of expectation, affect, and belief across individuals. This predicts measurable convergence of expectation or affect within groups exposed to a shared ambiguous situation, beyond what independent, uncoupled loops would produce.

#### 4.7 Altered States and Simulation Dominance

The character of conscious experience is determined by the balance between sensory contradiction and internally generated simulation. In ordinary waking perception, matched sensory input continuously checks active simulations. When this contradiction-checking input is reduced or absent — in dreaming, deep absorption, imagination, and other altered states — simulation content increasingly dominates the integrated state with comparatively little correction. The distinctive phenomenology of these states is a direct consequence of this shift in loop dominance, not evidence of a separate cognitive architecture.

### 5.0 Limitations and Future Development

TFL's central claims are not yet reducible to a single number or a single experiment, and several open problems remain. Affective intensity, while defined here as the persistence gain-control variable, is described conceptually rather than computationally, and turning E(t) into something measurable across contexts as different as a fleeting irritation and a years-long grief is a substantial undertaking. Simulation strength is, at present, inferred from recurrence and its influence on ambiguity-sensitive behavior rather than directly observed, and distinguishing a dormant, latent loop from one that has permanently resolved remains an open methodological problem. Between-system variation is understood as differences in how readily simulations persist, how quickly they decay, and how strongly contradiction redirects them — differences that are meaningful only if the same underlying content can be tracked with reasonable consistency across instances.

TFL is a mechanistic account of recursive integration across sensory input, retrieved memory content, predictive simulation, affective persistence, and the integrated current state, and of how that integration expresses itself across timescales, individuals, and states. By itself, it does not specify exact neural circuits, anatomical substrates, neurotransmitter systems, or oscillatory signatures, nor does it on its own explain long-term memory consolidation, developmental trajectories, or the full richness of emotional experience. Affective intensity is modeled here in the form required for persistence and reentry, not as a complete theory of emotion. The framework is a structural account of recursive, affect-modulated simulation. It does not explain why awareness exists or how subjectivity arises, and it does not prescribe therapeutic methods, behavioral interventions, or clinical protocols.

The predictions stated in Section 4 — identity convergence under repeated self-referential confirmation, recurrence reduction through observational closure, group-level synchronization of expectation and affect, and simulation dominance under reduced contradiction input — define the framework's primary empirical program. Precisely locating the loop's neural implementation, quantifying its dynamics in social and altered-state conditions, and determining whether an artificial system instantiates the same qualitative dynamics remain matters for future, more granular investigation.

None of these open questions are treated as weaknesses to be hidden. They are the framework's growing edge — the places where TFL is most likely to be refined as further work is done, and therefore the places most worth pursuing first.

### 6.0 Final Synthesis

The Temporal Feedback Loop proposes that continuity of experience, belief formation, persistent thought, delayed motivation, identity construction, observational learning, social influence, and altered-state phenomenology emerge from a single recursive architecture. Sensory input, reactivated memory, and a content-identifiable predictive simulation are integrated cycle after cycle into a representation of the present moment; affective intensity governs how long that representation survives; and belief, as a stabilized structural prior, shapes what each new cycle retrieves and anticipates.

Within this framework, identity is persistent self-referential simulation, belief is stabilized simulation structure, intrusive thought is unresolved simulation recurrence, observational closure is confirmation delivered through equivalent events, social influence is cross-system simulation coupling, and altered states are shifts in the balance between simulation and contradiction. These are not independent psychological processes. They are manifestations of one recursive loop expressed under different conditions of persistence, affective intensity, contradiction exposure, and simulation content.

TFL therefore offers a unified account of cognition in which memory, prediction, affect, belief, behavior, and experience are continuously reorganized through recursive feedback across time — a constrained, falsifiable architecture intended to be tested, refined, and built upon.
