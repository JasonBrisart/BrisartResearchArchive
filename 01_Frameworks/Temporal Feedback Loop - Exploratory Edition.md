### Abstract

The continuity of conscious experience across time is one of the deepest open problems in the study of mind. The Temporal Feedback Loop (TFL) proposes that this continuity is not given, but generated — produced moment to moment by a recursive process that integrates current sensory input, reactivated memory, and a content-identifiable predictive simulation into a single integrated state, cycle after cycle, for as long as the system remains active. Affective intensity is registered on the same cycle but is not one of the ingredients of that integration; instead, it separately determines how strongly the resulting representation will persist, recur, and resist dissolution once integration has already fixed what it contains. A simulation that carries enough affective weight does not simply appear and vanish; it becomes an open loop, propagating forward through successive cycles until it is closed by action, by confirmation from the world, or by the quiet erosion of its own affective charge.

TFL treats this recursive integration as the generative substrate of felt experience itself. What is experienced as "the present moment" is not a passive readout of the senses; it is the output of a synthesis of sensory evidence, memory-based expectation, and the currently active simulation — recomputed every cycle and carried forward as the seed of the next, with affective intensity determining only how long the result survives. Belief, on this view, is not a separate faculty sitting apart from perception; it is a stabilized, affectively reinforced structural prior operating across cycles rather than within any single one of them, durable enough to shape what memories are retrieved and what futures are treated as plausible when input is ambiguous.

The core mechanism is deliberately narrow: five variables, one integration function, one persistence rule, and one structural prior. Around that narrow core, this edition develops a wider set of candidate extensions — that loop resolution might, in principle, be achieved not only through the agent's own action but through affectively recognized observation of an equivalent event; that repeated, affectively supported self-referential simulation might contribute to something resembling identity; that loops in different individuals might, under some conditions, become partially coupled; and that states in which ordinary contradiction-checking input is reduced might predictably shift the loop's balance toward simulation-dominated content. These are offered explicitly as extensions beyond the core mechanism, worth testing on their own terms, and not as claims already established by the core loop itself.

### 1.0 Introduction

Why does experience feel continuous when its underlying substrate — neural firing, sensory sampling, attentional allocation — is anything but continuous? Why do certain thoughts return uninvited, hours or days after they seemed to have passed? Why does a belief, once formed, begin to reshape what a person notices, remembers, and expects, in a way that makes the belief harder to dislodge each time it is confirmed? And why does a persistent thought sometimes fade quietly while at other times it seems to sharpen and change shape the moment it collides with evidence that does not fit? These are not three separate puzzles. TFL proposes that they are three surface expressions of one recursive mechanism.

The core intuition is this: cognition does not process information once and discard it. It recycles. Sensory input, retrieved memory, and a content-identifiable predictive simulation are integrated into a working representation of the present moment, and that representation does not disappear once the moment has passed — it becomes the prior for the next cycle. Whether it survives into that next cycle at all is governed separately by how much affective charge it carries: enough charge, and it resurfaces, biases downstream interpretation of ambiguous input toward itself, and pulls behavior toward outcomes that would confirm it; too little, and it decays quickly, leaving almost no trace. The felt sense of a continuous, coherent stream of experience is what this recursive carry-over feels like from the inside.

TFL's founding commitments are as follows. First, affective intensity is the variable that determines whether a given simulation survives from one cycle to the next — it is the gain control on persistence, and nothing more; it does not decide what the simulation contains. Second, belief operates as a structural, affectively stabilized prior that reaches backward into memory retrieval and forward into simulation content, constraining both toward self-confirmation when the incoming input is ambiguous. Third, contradiction is what redirects an already-active loop: as contradicting evidence accumulates against a persistent simulation, the loop is pushed toward either revising that simulation's content or suppressing its recurrence outright, rather than toward some third, unspecified outcome. Fourth, none of this requires positing extra hidden machinery beyond the interaction of sensory input, memory, affect, and simulation already on the table, and none of it is worth taking seriously unless it can be shown to outperform simpler accounts built only from uncertainty, generic arousal, or habituation.

### 2.0 Core Mechanism

#### 2.1 Memory Reactivation as the Engine of Context

The hippocampus does not store static records; it reconstructs. Every act of remembering is closer to reconstruction than replay, drawing on distributed traces and reassembling them in light of the present moment's demands. Within TFL, this reconstructive capacity is what supplies Minfo(t) — the contextually relevant memory content that grounds a given cycle's simulation in something more than raw sensory input. Compressed, accelerated reactivation of prior sequences during quiet wakefulness and rest is a plausible substrate for how simulations gain their specific, situationally appropriate content rather than remaining generic anticipations. The more precisely a memory trace can be reactivated, the more precisely the resulting simulation can be shaped — which is one reason emotionally significant memories, encoded with unusual vividness, tend to generate simulations that feel unusually concrete and compelling.

#### 2.2 Integration as the Site of Synthesis

Somewhere in the system, sensory evidence, reactivated memory, and the active predictive simulation must be brought together into one workable representation — a synthesis rather than a mere concatenation. TFL locates this synthesis function at the point where distributed cortical and subcortical signals converge into a single integrated state, denoted F(t). This integrative site does not merely aggregate; it assigns relative weight to Input(t), Minfo(t), and Sim(t), resolves conflicts between what is expected and what is sensed, and produces the representation that will be carried forward as the next cycle's starting prior. Affective intensity is not one of the integration function's arguments — it is registered alongside integration, on the same cycle, but it does not enter the weighting that produces F(t) and does not supply representational content to it. What integration decides is what the moment contains; what affective intensity decides, separately, is how long that content survives.

#### 2.3 Affective Salience as Gain Control on Persistence

Affective intensity — denoted E(t) — is the variable that determines how loudly a given cycle's content will echo forward, but its office is deliberately narrow: it governs persistence and nothing else. It does not enter the integration that produces F(t), and it does not determine which simulation is generated or what that simulation predicts — those are settled by sensory evidence, reactivated memory, and the belief-based prior described below. High-salience content is more likely to survive from one cycle into the next, more readily reactivated after a quiet interval, and more resistant to extinction than low-salience content carrying the same informational payload. A simulation with negligible affective charge is integrated, produces its momentary effect, and is gone. A simulation with substantial affective charge becomes an attractor — it recurs, it resists competing input, and it can remain latent for extended periods before reactivating under conditions of contextual overlap, fatigue, or attentional drift — but in every case, what recurs is content that was already fixed by integration, not content that affect itself invented. Keeping this boundary intact is what allows persistence to be studied as a distinct phenomenon from the question of what, exactly, is persisting.

#### 2.4 Simulation as Forward-Directed Construction

The system does not wait passively for the future to arrive; it constructs forward models continuously, using memory and current context as raw material. Within TFL this is formalized as Sim(t) — a predictive representation shaped by Input(t), Minfo(t), and the prior integrated state F(t-1), and fed back into the next cycle's integration. Affective intensity does not shape what Sim(t) anticipates; it only governs whether that anticipation, once generated, is carried forward. The same architecture that allows a person to remember a past afternoon allows them to pre-experience an afternoon that has not yet happened, which is why imagined futures and remembered pasts share so much of their phenomenal texture. TFL treats this shared machinery as evidence that simulation is not a special add-on faculty but the same reconstructive process as memory, simply pointed forward instead of backward. Crucially, these simulations persist recursively — they do not resolve automatically simply because time has passed. They wait.

#### 2.5 Belief as a Structural Prior

Belief is not a momentary signal computed fresh on every cycle; it is a stabilized, affectively reinforced constraint that operates across cycles rather than within any single one of them. Once a belief has become durable enough, it biases which memories are reinstated as Minfo(t) and which candidate simulations are treated as plausible when Sim(t) is generated under ambiguous input. Belief does not add a sixth variable to the integration function — it conditions how the existing variables are populated, in the same way a lens does not appear inside a photograph but determines what the photograph can show. This is what allows belief to explain why two systems facing the same ambiguous input can integrate that input into markedly different simulations, without requiring that belief itself be treated as one more momentary ingredient of F(t).

#### 2.6 Toward a Wider Substrate

Nothing in the core mechanism as stated in Sections 2.1 through 2.5 is restricted to a single substrate. Any system capable of integrating time-indexed input, reactivated memory, and a content-identifiable predictive simulation, with a separate affect-analogous signal governing persistence and a structural prior conditioning retrieval under ambiguity, should in principle exhibit the same qualitative dynamics — persistence, escalation, delayed reentry, and contradiction-driven revision or suppression — regardless of whether that system is biological or artificial. Whether reduced external constraint, as in dreaming or deep absorption, corresponds to conditions under which the same loop operates with its ordinary contradiction-checking input loosened is a further question taken up as a candidate extension in Section 4.7, not a claim carried by the core mechanism itself.

### 3.0 Internal Processing Dynamics

#### 3.1 Formal Components of the Loop

The loop operates over discrete temporal windows and is defined by five interacting variables, restated here with their canonical roles for direct reference:

| Symbol | Definition | Role |
|---|---|---|
| `Input(t)` | Sensory information available at the current moment | Integration input |
| `Minfo(t)` | Memory content reactivated and made relevant to the current moment | Integration input |
| `Sim(t)` | Predictive simulation constructed from Input(t), Minfo(t), and the prior integrated state | Integration input |
| `E(t)` | Affective-autonomic intensity at the current moment | Persistence control only — not an integration input |
| `F(t)` | Fully integrated representation of the current cycle, carried forward as the next cycle's prior | Output of integration |
| Belief prior | Affectively stabilized structural constraint on retrieval and simulation under ambiguity | Cross-cycle constraint, not a momentary variable |

Of these five, only Input(t), Minfo(t), and Sim(t) enter the integration function. E(t) is registered on the same cycle but is not an argument of that function:

```text
F(t) = Integration[Input(t), Minfo(t), Sim(t)]
```

```text
Persistence of Sim(t) into the next cycle depends on E(t)
```

Integration here denotes the synthesis of perceptual input and memory-based expectation with the currently active simulation into one workable present-moment state, which then seeds retrieval and simulation in the following cycle. A belief-based prior constrains this process across cycles without being represented as an additional momentary function of E(t).

#### 3.2 The Four Phases of a Loop Cycle

**Encoding.** A stimulus, thought, or belief enters the system. If it arrives with elevated affective charge, it is preferentially encoded, and the magnitude of that charge governs the probability of later reactivation.

**Integration.** The encoded content is fused with reactivated memory and current sensory input, producing a forward simulation that may operate beneath conscious notice or may be explicitly imagined.

**Propagation.** If the simulation remains unresolved, it continues to circulate. It may intensify, adapt, or lie dormant depending on ongoing input and internal shifts, and it can do so across timescales ranging from sub-second micro-loops to reactivation that surfaces only after days or longer.

**Resolution.** The loop closes when the simulation is behaviorally enacted, when the environment confirms it, when its affective charge decays below the threshold needed to sustain it, or when accumulating contradiction pushes the loop toward revision or suppression. Once resolved, the representation is discharged, attention reallocates, and the system is available for new cycles.

#### 3.3 Contradiction: Revision Versus Suppression

Disconfirmation is not a single event but a matter of degree, and the loop can respond to it in one of two structurally distinct ways. In **revision**, the active simulation's content changes to better accommodate the contradicting evidence while the loop itself remains open — the anticipated outcome is edited rather than abandoned. In **suppression**, the active simulation stops recurring without being replaced by a revised version — the loop closes not because it was satisfied but because it was overridden. Which of the two occurs is expected to depend jointly on how much affective support the simulation still carries and how strongly the belief prior favors reinterpretation over abandonment: well-supported, belief-congruent simulations tend toward revision under mild contradiction, while weakly supported ones tend toward outright suppression under the same pressure. Distinguishing these two outcomes matters, because a system that only ever suppressed under contradiction, never revised, would not need a simulation-generating mechanism at all — a much simpler on/off gate would do the same work.

#### 3.4 Stability, Escalation, and Resolution

A loop is stable when its content recurs across cycles without meaningful change — the same anticipated outcome, reasserting itself with roughly constant force. A loop escalates when recurrence, affective intensity, or behavioral pull increases across successive cycles, typically because each partial confirmation feeds back into the belief structure that generated the simulation in the first place, deepening the attractor. A loop resolves when one of the closure conditions above is met, at which point the affective charge that sustained it is free to dissipate.

For direct reference, the conditions governing continuation versus resolution are as follows. **Continuation conditions** — a loop persists into further cycles when affective intensity remains elevated, when the active simulation remains unresolved or is re-triggered by context, and when reactivated memory continues to reinforce a congruent simulation. **Resolution conditions** — a loop closes when the simulation is behaviorally enacted, when it is externally confirmed through matched sensory input, when affective intensity decays below the level needed to sustain it, or when cognitive reinterpretation dissolves the simulation's original force.

#### 3.5 Delayed Reentry

A loop need not resolve quickly, and it need not announce its return. Content that has gone quiet can reactivate later — sometimes much later — when context overlaps sufficiently with the conditions of original encoding, or when affective readiness happens to align. This is why a thought that seemed to pass can resurface as unplanned motivation, and why unresolved emotional material can reassert itself years after the event that generated it, triggered by nothing more than an incidental cue.

#### 3.6 Variable Closure and Boundary Conditions

TFL applies only where predictive content remains distinguishable, affective intensity can plausibly govern persistence, and contradiction can meaningfully alter the course of the loop. It does not extend beyond the declared variables of sensory input, retrieved memory content, predictive simulation, affective intensity, the integrated current state, and belief-based prior constraint. Where these conditions fall away — under chaotic input, in the absence of any identifiable simulation content, or under extreme physiological disruption — the loop's explanatory reach becomes limited, and further phenomena should not be silently absorbed into the model by inventing additional undeclared variables.

#### 3.7 Distinguishing the Loop from Simpler Accounts

None of the above is interesting unless it can be told apart from three much cheaper explanations. A system that simply carries generic uncertainty forward, a system that is merely aroused without any content-specific memory, and a system that is only repeating a habituated response can each mimic persistence, bias, and even something resembling reentry without any recursive simulation doing the work. TFL earns its distinctiveness only where recurrence, ambiguity-driven bias, delayed reentry, and contradiction-driven revision or suppression remain tied to identifiable simulation content and to affective intensity that is not reducible to sensory uncertainty or nonspecific arousal. Wherever these phenomena are fully explained by uncertainty alone, by arousal alone, or by habit alone, the loop architecture is not doing any explanatory work in that instance, however naturally the narrative language above might seem to fit.

### 4.0 Applications and Predictions

#### 4.1 Persistent Thought and Simulation Reentry

Thoughts, intentions, and hypothetical narratives are simulations like any other, and their fate is governed by the same affective gain control as everything else in the loop. Low-salience simulations decay quickly and leave little trace. High-salience simulations persist, bias attention and memory retrieval toward themselves, alter the interpretation of ambiguous input in their own favor, and lie latent as priors capable of reasserting themselves at any later point where context permits. An unresolved emotional charge functions, functionally speaking, as an open loop — something the system keeps circling back to until it is closed.

#### 4.2 Belief as a Self-Confirming Structural Encoding

Belief is not a detached cognitive judgment; it is an affectively stabilized prior woven into the loop's ongoing operation. Once a belief has been reinforced enough times with sufficient affective backing, it begins to bias memory retrieval toward confirmatory content, constrain simulated futures toward belief-consistent outcomes, and filter ambiguous incoming information so that confirmatory readings are favored. This produces a recursive, self-confirming dynamic: belief shapes perception, and perception reaffirms belief, cycle after cycle, until belief-congruent simulations come to dominate the loop's ongoing content almost entirely, regardless of how proportionate that dominance is to the evidence actually available.

#### 4.3 Identity Convergence

The core loop, on its own, explains the persistence and updating of a single simulation. It does not by itself explain identity. What it can plausibly supply is one ingredient such an explanation would need: if a self-referential simulation is generated repeatedly, receives repeated affective support, and is repeatedly closed by behavior or feedback that confirms rather than contradicts it, the same persistence-and-reentry dynamics described above would predict that this particular content recurs far more reliably than an ordinary unsupported simulation. Whether that recurring pattern is sufficient to constitute identity, or merely one input among others that a separate identity-level account would need to integrate, is a question the core mechanism does not settle on its own. It is offered here as a candidate extension — an embedding of TFL within a broader account of identity — rather than as a claim that TFL alone explains how a self is formed.

#### 4.4 Distributed and Observational Resolution

The resolution conditions defined in Section 3 are stated in terms of behavioral enactment, environmental confirmation, affective decay, or cognitive reinterpretation. A further candidate extension is that environmental confirmation need not be confirmation of the agent's own situation: if an observed, described, or imagined event is affectively recognized as equivalent to an active simulation's anticipated outcome, it may be able to satisfy the same confirmation role that direct experience would otherwise have filled. This would predict that vicarious exposure to an equivalent event can measurably reduce recurrence of a matched simulation, in a manner distinguishable from mere forgetting or distraction. This remains an extension of the resolution conditions rather than a component of the core mechanism, and it would need to be shown that recognition-based closure behaves differently from ordinary decay before it could be treated as established.

#### 4.5 Escalating and Intrusive Loops

When none of the resolution conditions in Section 3.2 are met — no enactment, no confirmation, no affective decay, no reinterpretation — the persistence mechanism predicts that the simulation simply continues to recur rather than resolving on its own. If the surrounding context repeatedly supplies partial or ambiguous input that neither confirms nor clearly contradicts the active simulation, each unresolved cycle has an opportunity to reinforce rather than weaken affective support for it. This offers a candidate account of intrusive and recurring thought as an ordinary consequence of the persistence mechanism operating without an available closure condition, rather than as a departure from it.

#### 4.6 Loops Beyond the Individual

Section 4.4 proposes that an observed event can, in principle, satisfy a resolution condition for the observer's own simulation. A further and more speculative extension is that this same mechanism could operate in more than one direction at once within a group: if several individuals hold affectively similar unresolved simulations, and each individual's behavior functions as potential confirming or contradicting input for the others, their loops could in principle become partially coupled. This would predict measurable synchronization of expectation or affect within groups exposed to a shared ambiguous situation, beyond what independent, uncoupled loops would produce. This extension reaches well past anything the core mechanism was built to describe on its own, and it is offered here strictly as a direction worth testing rather than as a claim the framework currently supports.

#### 4.7 Altered and Non-Ordinary States

The boundary conditions in Section 3 assume that contradiction from matched sensory input is generally available to check an active simulation. A further candidate extension is that states in which this contradiction-checking input is reduced or absent — dreaming, deep absorption, or certain other altered states — would predict a shift toward simulation content dominating the integrated state with comparatively little correction from matched sensory evidence. Under this reading, the distinctive phenomenology of such states would be a predicted consequence of reduced contradiction input rather than evidence of a separate mechanism. This prediction has not been tested here and is offered only as a direction the boundary-condition logic already implicit in Section 3 would naturally extend toward.

### 5.0 Limitations and Future Development

TFL's central claims are not yet reducible to a single number or a single experiment, and several open problems remain squarely unresolved. Affective intensity, while treated here as a persistence-only gain-control variable, is described conceptually rather than computationally, and turning E(t) into something measurable across contexts as different as a fleeting irritation and a years-long grief is a substantial undertaking rather than a footnote. Simulation strength is, at present, inferred from recurrence and its influence on ambiguity-sensitive behavior rather than directly observed, and distinguishing a genuinely dormant, latent loop from one that has simply and permanently resolved remains an open methodological problem rather than a solved one. Between-system variation should be understood only as differences in how readily simulations persist, how quickly they decay, and how strongly contradiction redirects them — differences that are meaningful only if the same underlying content can still be tracked with reasonable consistency across instances.

TFL is a local mechanistic account of short-timescale recursive integration across sensory input, retrieved memory content, predictive simulation, affective persistence, and the integrated current state. By itself, it does not specify exact neural circuits, anatomical substrates, neurotransmitter systems, or oscillatory signatures, nor does it on its own explain large-scale network transitions, long-term memory consolidation, developmental trajectories, or the full richness of emotional experience. Affective intensity is modeled here only in the minimal form required for persistence and reentry, not as a complete theory of emotion or phenomenology. The framework should be read as a structural account of recursive, affect-modulated simulation. It does not explain what consciousness is, why awareness exists, or how subjectivity arises, and it does not attribute agency, volition, goals, or selfhood to internal processes. Nor does it prescribe therapeutic methods, behavioral interventions, or clinical protocols.

Sections 4.3, 4.4, 4.6, and 4.7 are explicitly candidate extensions rather than components of the core mechanism, and they are stated with more narrative confidence than current evidence can discharge. They are offered to generate testable predictions, not to stand as settled conclusions, and none of them are required for the core loop described in Sections 2 and 3 to remain valid. Precisely locating the loop's neural implementation, and determining whether an artificial system could instantiate the same qualitative dynamics, remain matters for future, more granular investigation rather than claims made here.

None of these open questions are treated as weaknesses to be hidden. They are the framework's growing edge — the places where TFL is most likely to be revised, extended, or overturned as further work is done, and therefore the places most worth pursuing first.

### 6.0 Final Synthesis

TFL proposes that the felt continuity of experience, the stubborn persistence of certain thoughts, and the self-reinforcing grip of belief are not separate phenomena requiring separate explanations. They are what one recursive architecture — sensory input and reactivated memory integrated with a content-identifiable predictive simulation, cycle after cycle, under the separate gain control of affective intensity — looks like when it is examined at different timescales, different affective intensities, and different points of resolution. Its defining feature is not the presence of prediction, memory, or affect individually, but the recursive persistence of identifiable simulation content across temporally structured integration cycles, with affect governing survival rather than content.

Identity convergence, observational and distributed resolution, and altered-state phenomenology are offered here as candidate extensions of that same core — directions the mechanism plausibly reaches toward, not conclusions it has already established. TFL is presented not as a finished account, but as a constrained, falsifiable core intended to be pushed, tested, extended, and, where its candidate extensions fail, revised without disturbing the mechanism itself.
