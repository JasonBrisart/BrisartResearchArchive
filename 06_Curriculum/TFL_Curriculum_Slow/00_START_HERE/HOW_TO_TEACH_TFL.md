# How to Teach TFL

## 1. The one-sentence version

> What I notice, what I remember, and what I expect combine into right now — and
> right now shapes what comes next.

## 2. Teach the loop before the parts

Session one of every grade establishes that the loop exists. The parts are taught
as answers to a question students already have: what exactly is repeating?

## 3. Experience first, language second

Create the experience. Ask what happened in the student's words. Name it in the
framework's words. Return to the experience using the new name. Never swap the
first and third steps.

## 4. The surprise is the instrument

Prediction is invisible when it succeeds and visible the moment it fails. The move
after any mismatch is always: "You were surprised. Surprised means you expected
something. What did you expect?"

## 5. Separate how long it lasted from whether it was right

Students of every age arrive assuming a prediction that felt strong was correct.
Grades 1 to 5 plant the distinction informally. Grade 7 tests it directly on the
student's own data.

## 6. Match formality to the grade

Never push notation downward. A second grader writing `Sim(t)` has learned
handwriting. A twelfth grader saying "the expecting part" has been under-taught.

## 7. Wrong predictions are ungraded

Model it. Predict aloud, be wrong, say so cheerfully.

## 8. Handle affect carefully

Games, weather, schedules, sport, everyday surprises. Never personal emotional
history, never diagnostic framing.

## 9. Scope is content, not a disclaimer

Teach the question: "Is that something this model is for?"

## 10. End every session by propagating forward

Close with one sentence that opens the next session. Each homework ends by asking
for that sentence from memory, which is what protects the step.
